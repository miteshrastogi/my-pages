01

Rare loss needs explicit scenarios

Why experience is not enough

A catastrophe model makes rare-loss scenarios explicit; it does not make them certain.

The data problem comes first

Catastrophe modelling begins with a practical limit of experience rating. The IAA Risk Book says standard historical-claims methods are inappropriate for most catastrophes; the American Academy of Actuaries contrasts this with reasonably predictable, frequent losses, for which recent experience can support projection. Synthesis. A catastrophe model addresses that gap by examining a constructed set of possible events and consequences. It expands the scenarios available for a decision without making the uncertain future certain. [partial-access]

Synthesis. The useful contrast is therefore not “data versus models.” Historical events, scientific knowledge, engineering relationships and portfolio data all inform the model. The change is in how they are assembled: instead of waiting for enough catastrophes to appear in a claims triangle, the practitioner evaluates a deliberately constructed range of rare scenarios and their loss implications. [partial-access]

Two events changed industry practice

The supplied sources consistently place Hurricane Andrew in 1992 and the Northridge earthquake in 1994 at the centre of the industry shift. The March 2025 NAIC primer says insurers had depended only on historical claims experience before Andrew and that both events led actuaries to recognise the value of probabilistic computer simulation for estimating severe-event losses. It also says Northridge ended an experience-only approach to California earthquake assessment. The 2015 IAA account describes adoption as accelerating after the two events; the pack provides no adoption percentage or insurer count. [partial-access]

Andrew also illustrates why model output must remain open to challenge. The 2018 Academy monograph reports a modeller's $13 billion estimate within hours of landfall and a reported Andrew loss of $15.5 billion. Synthesis. The available passage leaves the early estimate’s loss basis unstated. Numerical proximity alone therefore cannot establish accuracy on a common basis. For Katrina in 2005, Marsh reported that initial model-based damage estimates were about one-quarter of an eventual insured loss above $41 billion (the source supplies that rounded fraction rather than the underlying estimates), then concluded that catastrophe models need constant updating. Synthesis. Real events can validate parts of a model while exposing missing mechanisms, weak assumptions or changing exposure. [partial-access]

Forward-looking does not mean certain

A model gives rare events a disciplined representation: scenarios have characteristics and occurrence rates, and their effects are calculated against a specified portfolio. Synthesis. The resulting distribution provides a way to compare scenarios beyond the observed claims record. Yet each stage depends on choices about event generation, local intensity, building response, values and contractual terms. The NAIC primer warns that inaccurate or materially incomplete inputs increase output uncertainty; a Geneva Association report notes that real events have revealed model limitations and the importance of understanding assumptions. [partial-access]

Synthesis. For practice, a model result is conditional: it describes losses within the model's event set, data, assumptions and financial structure. That makes it suitable for disciplined decisions, not automatic belief. Chapter 2 follows those conditions through the calculation, while later chapters separate the different uncertainty concepts and the metrics drawn from the loss distribution. [partial-access]

The reasoning can travel, with boundaries

The underlying problem is correlated loss: one event can affect many separately written contracts at once. A 2008 industry guide applied that logic beyond property catastrophe, reporting that RMS began pandemic modelling in 2004 and developed a probabilistic pandemic influenza model in 2006–2007. The same guide gave a longevity-related pension illustration dated to 2008. These are dated examples and proposals, not evidence in the supplied pack of broad later adoption across life, health or pensions. [partial-access]

Other sources extend the idea more cautiously. A 2018 Geneva Association report described transferring catastrophe-modelling technology to public-sector disaster management, development planning and sovereign risk transfer; a 2025 conference deck presented insurance experience as a possible playbook for banks and regulators. Synthesis. These statements show portability of the reasoning, not proof that one property model or its calibration transfers unchanged. The transferable habit is to make low-frequency scenarios, dependencies and consequences explicit, then test whether the evidence and structure fit the new decision. [partial-access]

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

Sparse catastrophe experience needs a scenario-based view.

Andrew and Northridge accelerated adoption, while Katrina exposed the need for revision.

Models structure uncertainty; they do not remove it.

02

From event intensity to insured loss

Inside the model: follow the calculation

Loss emerges from event hazard, exposure, vulnerability and financial terms acting together.

Use four modules as a working map

A practical way to read a catastrophe model is to separate four jobs: hazard, exposure, vulnerability and financial calculation. The March 2025 NAIC primer and the Geneva Association use this four-module arrangement. It is a working map, not universal vocabulary. Other supplied sources count three modules, treat exposure as an input rather than a module, or separate stochastic event generation from hazard. The NAIC itself notes that vendor terminology varies. Synthesis. When comparing models, inspect what each component does and passes onward rather than matching labels alone. [partial-access]

The calculation has an order

The hazard calculation starts with a probabilistic event set: simulated scenarios described by features such as strength, location, path and annual occurrence rate. For each event it produces a footprint showing how intensity varies across the affected area. “Intensity” is the local physical condition relevant to the peril—for example, wind speed, inundation depth or ground motion—not a loss amount. [partial-access]

The exposure data describe what is at risk and where. Depending on the implementation, they include locations, replacement values, construction, occupancy, height, year built and policy terms. Synthesis. Location helps connect an asset to the hazard footprint; physical attributes are needed to estimate its response; values and contract terms are needed later to express damage as money and insured loss. The NAIC groups these records in an exposure module, while other frameworks distribute them as inputs to several calculations. [partial-access]

The vulnerability calculation combines local hazard intensity with the physical characteristics of the exposed asset. Damage functions translate that combination into expected physical damage, often represented as a mean damage ratio with uncertainty around it. Synthesis. Treat mean damage ratio as an intermediate damage measure. The financial calculation later expresses damage in currency and applies contract terms. [partial-access]

The financial calculation expresses damage in monetary terms and applies the relevant contract. The NAIC primer describes the module as translating physical damage into a dollar loss, then applying conditions such as deductibles and limits. Reinsurance may then transform the result again. Event losses are aggregated into a loss probability distribution, from which the metrics introduced in Chapter 4 are read. [partial-access]

Keep four quantities distinct

Synthesis. Confusion often begins when adjacent outputs are all called “loss.” Hazard intensity describes the event at a location. The damage ratio describes physical response relative to an asset value. Monetary damage expresses that physical effect in currency. Insured loss is the amount after specified policy conditions; ceded or net views require further reinsurance calculations. A chart or file should therefore name both the quantity and the financial perspective. [partial-access]

Illustration. Take one assumed damage outcome: a 20% damage ratio for a building with a stated replacement value of $1 million. Multiplying those illustrative inputs gives $200,000 of monetary damage. A $50,000 deductible and a sufficiently high policy limit would then produce $150,000 under that simplified policy calculation. This is a single-outcome illustration, not expected insured loss calculated from mean damage. When damage is uncertain, transform each possible loss before averaging; Chapter 5 shows why that order matters. Actual models apply their own coverage structures and aggregation rules. [partial-access]

Exposure connects the stages

Synthesis. The model is an ordered calculation with shared inputs, rather than four isolated boxes. Exposure location helps select local hazard intensity; building attributes shape vulnerability; values and terms feed the financial result. This is one-way dependency, not feedback from loss to hazard. The supplied sources do not explicitly call the architecture a dependency graph, but their input descriptions show exposure entering several stages. [partial-access]

Synthesis. That structure also explains why data quality propagates. A location error can select the wrong intensity; a construction error can select the wrong damage relationship; an incorrect value or deductible can distort the insured result even if the hazard science is sound. The NAIC primer says inaccurate or materially incomplete input data increase model-output uncertainty, and the 2024 technical paper warns that every step can introduce significant uncertainty into gross and net projections. Diagnose a surprising result at the stage where its evidence enters. [partial-access]

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

Hazard creates intensity, vulnerability creates damage, and finance creates insured loss.

Exposure data feed several stages of the ordered calculation.

Module names vary, so compare functions and hand-offs.

03

Name the hazard relationship, market usage and model scope before comparing results.

Peril labels are not a maturity scale

Peril labels are context-dependent, while data and model maturity must be assessed peril by peril.

Start with the relationship between hazards

In technical model documentation, a secondary peril often means a hazard that follows or is amplified by a primary event. Examples in the supplied sources include storm surge, liquefaction and fire following earthquake; another source explicitly calls these cascading effects. In that sense, secondary describes the causal chain, not commercial importance, loss frequency or model quality. [partial-access]

The term must also be kept separate from secondary uncertainty. That phrase concerns the range of possible losses after a particular event has occurred. It describes uncertainty in conditional loss, not a class of hazards. [partial-access]

Treat market shorthand as shorthand

The supplied pack also shows secondary peril used informally in a discussion of severe convective storm. The supplied NAIC primer uses the phrase while discussing severe convective storms, but does not define a primary-versus-secondary taxonomy. The pack therefore does not support treating that split as a universal technical classification. [partial-access]

Synthesis. When the informal sense is intended, say so and name the actual peril. “Flood model” or “severe-convective-storm model” carries more information than “secondary-peril model.” Reserve cascading peril for storm surge, liquefaction, fire following earthquake and comparable dependencies. This vocabulary prevents a market label from being mistaken for a claim about causation or reliability. [partial-access]

Assess maturity one peril and territory at a time

Synthesis. Model maturity reflects the history of model development, the available observations and the stability of results. A 2018 American Academy of Actuaries monograph reports that hurricane and earthquake models entered the market before severe-convective-storm, wildfire and flood models. The March 2025 NAIC primer likewise says hurricane and earthquake models have existed longer than wildfire models, and describes their results as more consistent and less variable than those from less mature models. Crucially, the primer adds that less mature models are not thereby unreliable. [partial-access]

Those statements are dated and contextual, not permanent rankings. The same primer describes wildfire and flood models as less mature than other catastrophe models. An Australian technical paper from November 2024 uses a different market vocabulary: it says bushfire, flood, storm and hail were collectively known there as “non-modelled perils,” while noting that vendors offered some models that had not been universally accepted. The label reports a particular market history; it does not prove that a peril is literally unmodelled everywhere. [partial-access]

Synthesis. A useful review asks: Which peril and territory? Which hazard components and cascading effects are represented? What observations support calibration and validation? How much do credible models vary at the portfolio locations and loss levels relevant to the decision? Chapter 7 develops the comparison discipline; here the point is that age, acceptance and reliability are related questions, not synonyms.

Read loss trends without inventing a hazard trend

Recent insured-loss experience explains why frequent perils demand attention. A January 2026 report states that severe convective storms cost global insurers a combined USD208 billion in 2023–2025, in the report’s stated “today’s dollars” basis, and calls the peril a dominant annual loss driver. It also estimates that non-hazard-related drivers accounted for 80–90% of US severe-convective-storm loss growth since 2000. [partial-access]

The same report describes the 2025 US wildfire season as the costliest on record for insurers, above USD41 billion. That is a dated statement about US insured loss, not evidence that wildfire hazard, model quality or modeled tail contribution universally increased. [partial-access]

Synthesis. The supplied pack does not establish that one peril family dominates the modeled tail. It supports a narrower operating conclusion: loss relevance can rise faster than model maturity, and observed loss growth can reflect exposure and cost as well as hazard. Peril labels should begin the investigation, never finish it.

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

Define what a peril label means in context.

Judge maturity for the specific peril and territory.

Separate insured-loss trends from hazard trends.

04

Every catastrophe metric preserves a different feature of annual loss.

Read the distribution, not one number

AAL, OEP, AEP, return period, PML and TVaR answer different questions.

Begin with the annual loss distribution

Model runs produce event and year losses. An exceedance-probability curve relates loss thresholds to annual exceedance probabilities. OEP and AEP describe different annual variables; AAL, return-period losses, PML and TVaR summarize a declared distribution. [partial-access]

Synthesis. Before reading a point, declare the loss basis, occurrence or aggregate basis, portfolio and time period. Chapter 5 follows the financial transformations.

Build the curve from annual loss rows

Model output may be organized as an event loss table (ELT) or a year loss table (YLT); a supplied event-table example associates each event with an occurrence rate. [partial-access]

Synthesis. To build annual curves, retain which loss occurrences fall together in each simulated year under the model's occurrence and dependence assumptions. Then calculate that year's largest occurrence and total. Event rates alone do not identify which losses share a year.

Illustration. Take five invented, equally likely years, in monetary units. Keep the events together, then take either their largest loss or their annual sum. This arithmetic example is separate from Exhibit 02’s continuous distribution.

Year Event A Event B Largest Total
1 0 0 0 0
2 10 0 10 10
3 20 5 20 25
4 50 0 50 50

Mean 32 5 32 37

AAL is the annual-total mean, 37; the mean annual maximum is 32. At a loss threshold of 20, counting only losses strictly greater than 20, the largest occurrence exceeds it in 2 of 5 years (40%); the annual total exceeds it in 3 of 5 years (60%). Year 3 creates the difference: neither event exceeds 20, but their sum does. These are teaching probabilities, not empirical frequency estimates.

Synthesis. Repeat that count at each threshold and you obtain two EP curves. OEP and AEP are built from different columns of the same year rows. Choosing the column comes before reading a probability, return period or loss point. [partial-access]

OEP and AEP describe different random variables

Occurrence exceedance probability (OEP) is the chance that the largest single occurrence in a year exceeds a stated loss. Aggregate exceedance probability (AEP) is the chance that the sum of all event losses in the year exceeds it. At a given return period, the AEP loss is at least as large as the OEP loss because the annual total includes the largest occurrence and may include others. [partial-access]

This distinction fixes average annual loss (AAL). AAL is the long-run mean of annual total loss: theoretically it is calculated from the AEP distribution. It is not the mean annual maximum from OEP. The March 2025 NAIC primer calls AAL the expected value in any given year and its most common catastrophe pricing metric; an AAA monograph says AALs are additive while PMLs are not. [partial-access]

Synthesis. AAL compresses the annual distribution into its mean; portfolios with similar AALs can still have different tails. The AAA example reports similar AALs with different ratios of a 250-year PML to AAL. [partial-access]

The IAA describes OEP and AEP as often converging in the tail for very infrequent, severe events such as earthquakes, while frequent events such as tornado outbreaks can produce substantially higher aggregate loss. Synthesis. The practical mechanism is the number of losses accumulating in the same year; it is not a fixed ranking of perils. [partial-access]

The sources also warn that AALs add across books but PMLs and OEP/AEP curve points generally do not. Synthesis. Recompute the combined distribution with the books’ event dependence represented. Adding separate tail points assumes an alignment of extreme outcomes that the separate points do not establish. [partial-access]

Return period is probability language, not a timetable

A return period is the reciprocal of annual exceedance probability. It expresses annual probability in years; it does not schedule one event per block of years. The NAIC primer warns that a 100-year event need not occur precisely once every 100 years. [partial-access]

Probable maximum loss (PML) is best read as a loss amount paired with a probability or return period. The sources use different bases: an AAA monograph allows an event or a year, the NAIC primer describes a single event, and an Australian paper gives an OEP example. A PML must therefore state its curve, loss basis, probability and time frame. It is a curve point, despite the finality of the word maximum. [partial-access]

Tail averages require the full tail

Tail value at risk (TVaR) is the average loss conditional on loss reaching or exceeding a selected threshold. It uses losses throughout the selected tail, so it needs more than the PML point. [partial-access]

A PML hides what happens beyond its point. TVaR describes tail thickness but is conditional on the same modeled distribution. Synthesis. It does not repair events omitted from that distribution or errors in its inputs and assumptions. The AAL and tail metrics are based on the same exceedance-probability curve, so weaknesses in that curve can affect both. [partial-access]

Synthesis. First select the annual variable: OEP uses the largest occurrence; AEP uses the annual total. Then choose a reading:

Reading What it preserves What it does not tell you
AAL Mean annual total loss Which years are severe, or how large their losses can be
PML One loss amount at a declared annual probability How losses behave beyond that point
TVaR Average loss in the selected tail The full shape of that tail

In Exhibit 02, start at 100 years. Switch OEP/AEP while keeping the probability fixed, then move the threshold. Compare the PML point with the tail average rather than treating either as a maximum possible loss. The interactive uses a continuous teaching distribution; its methodology explains the separate issue of ties in discrete tables.

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

AAL is the mean annual total loss.

OEP and AEP describe different annual variables.

Return periods are probabilities, not schedules.

05

The same hazard can produce several financially valid answers.

Turn losses into decisions

Financial terms transform the losses, and each decision needs a declared loss basis and appropriate summaries.

Name the loss before using it

A loss number is incomplete until its financial viewpoint is named. Ground-up loss is the monetary damage before insurance policy terms. Insured loss is the amount produced after applying the modeled policy conditions, such as deductibles and limits; some sources call this gross loss from the insurer's viewpoint. Ceded loss is the portion assigned to reinsurance under the modeled reinsurance terms. Net loss is what remains with the insurer after modeled reinsurance or retrocession. The 2019 boot-camp material describes gross as loss after deductibles, limits and attachment points, and net as loss after treaty and facultative cessions; the 2024 Australian technical paper likewise describes policy features producing gross loss and reinsurance or retrocession producing net loss. [partial-access]

Synthesis. These labels are conventions, not self-executing definitions. A report should state whether expenses, demand surge, loss adjustment expense, reinstatement premiums, recoverability and other adjustments are included. In this guide, “insured” and “gross” refer to loss after modeled policy terms but before modeled reinsurance; “ceded” is the modeled reinsurance recovery; and “net” is gross less ceded. This declared simplification makes the layer illustration below auditable; it is an authored convention, not a sourced formula.

Apply the terms before taking the average

The IAA describes policy terms being applied to the secondary-uncertainty distributions after ground-up losses are calculated. The input is a range of possible loss outcomes, not merely the mean of that range. [partial-access]

Illustration. Conditional on one event occurring, suppose damage is either 0 or 100, each with probability 50%. The mean damage is 50. A deductible of 50, with no payment cap, produces:

Damage outcome Probability Insured payment after deductible
0 50% 0
100 50% 50
Expected value 25

Averaging the two payments gives 25. Applying the deductible once to mean damage gives 0: max(50 − 50, 0). That shortcut loses the high-damage outcome. The expected insured payment is therefore not generally the policy payment calculated on expected damage.

Synthesis. Limits and reinsurance layers are also nonlinear transformations. Apply the relevant terms to the loss outcomes on the contract’s occurrence or annual basis, then summarize the resulting distribution. The next example isolates that second choice of basis.

Occurrence and aggregate covers answer different questions

A per-occurrence layer responds to a contractually defined loss from one occurrence. Illustration: for a 100 monetary-unit layer attaching at 50, an occurrence loss of 120 produces 70 of ceded loss and leaves 50 net, assuming no other terms. That arithmetic is an authored teaching derivation. The occurrence exceedance probability curve is the relevant view for asking how likely an occurrence layer is to be reached; the 2024 technical paper says OEP is used to assess how likely loss will be ceded to a per-occurrence layer and in purchasing and pricing catastrophe reinsurance. [partial-access]

An annual aggregate cover instead responds after losses are accumulated over the contract year under its annual terms. Several individually small occurrences may therefore activate an aggregate cover even when no single occurrence reaches an occurrence attachment. The annual exceedance probability distribution is useful for annual aggregate stop-loss protection and reinstatement analysis. [partial-access]

Illustration — one layer, two contract bases. Keep the same 100-unit limit above an attachment of 50. The losses below are already on the layer's insured-loss basis. Assume a fresh full limit for each occurrence, with no annual cap, reinstatement cost or other terms:

Year’s losses Occurrence recovery Annual recovery
30 + 30 = 60 0 + 0 = 0 10
120 + 120 = 240 70 + 70 = 140 100

Aggregation lets small losses cross the attachment; repeated occurrence limits can provide more total recovery after large losses. Which cover pays more depends on the losses and terms. Exhibit 03 makes the same distinction in the continuous teaching model.

Synthesis. Do not apply an occurrence layer to the annual total or read an aggregate attachment from OEP unless the contract itself calls for that treatment. For either structure, calculate the loss distribution on the layer's own basis. Reinsurance pricing discussions may begin with the layer AAL plus a factor applied to its standard deviation, but that is a starting framework rather than a universal price rule. [partial-access]

Match the summaries to the decision

No decision owns a single metric. Pricing needs expected cost and a risk or capital allowance; a 2018 American Academy of Actuaries monograph gives the teaching relationship “Premium = AAL + Expense Load + Risk Load,” while the 2015 IAA chapter says insurers had moved beyond approaches based only on expected loss and standard deviation. [partial-access]

Underwriting and accumulation work asks how a proposed change affects the portfolio. The same 2018 monograph discusses the effect of adding or removing a property on portfolio PML and reviewing TVaR for the effect on extreme risk; it also warns that AALs are additive while PMLs are not. [partial-access]

Capital, solvency and rating review use tail points, but the required point and basis depend on the regime and purpose. The 2015 IAA chapter reports US reliance on 0.01 and 0.004 exceedance probabilities for capital, reinsurance and risk-tolerance decisions. The March 2025 NAIC primer—then marked pending adoption—describes 100-year PML outputs for a catastrophe risk charge, while a November 2024 Australian technical paper says 200 years is often relevant to its natural-perils capital requirement and reinsurance review. These are dated examples, not current universal thresholds. [partial-access]

Insurance-linked securities add another decision context: a 2018 monograph says catastrophe-model output supplies quantitative information used to develop catastrophe bonds and other risk-linked securities, while a 2008 guide identifies modeled loss as one trigger structure. Neither excerpt establishes a single required metric or trigger for all transactions. [partial-access]

Synthesis: every decision record should declare the loss viewpoint (ground-up, insured/gross, ceded or net), the time basis (occurrence or annual aggregate), the curve and statistic used, the modeled contract terms, and the date and jurisdiction of any external threshold. That declaration prevents a technically correct number from answering the wrong financial question.

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

Name the financial viewpoint before comparing losses.

Occurrence and aggregate protection require different loss constructions.

Use several summaries when the decision depends on both expected cost and tail risk.

06

Two classifications describe different features of the same modeling problem.

Two vocabularies for uncertainty

Event-versus-conditional-loss and randomness-versus-knowledge classify different aspects of uncertainty.

Primary and secondary identify what is uncertain

In catastrophe-model terminology, primary uncertainty concerns the events themselves: whether an event occurs. Secondary uncertainty concerns the amount of loss, given that an event has occurred. The IAA Risk Book gives the event-based definition of primary uncertainty, while 2019 reinsurance boot-camp slides state both definitions; a 2024 Australian technical paper also uses secondary uncertainty for loss given an event. [partial-access]

The model carries these two layers differently. Primary uncertainty appears through occurrence rates attached to events in a sampled catalog. The IAA chapter says event catalogs commonly contain large samples generated by Monte Carlo or stratified sampling, and the 2019 slides identify an occurrence rate for each event in an event-loss table. [partial-access]

Secondary uncertainty appears through variation around the conditional loss for an event. Even after the catalog event is selected, its local intensity and resulting damage can vary; the IAA includes uncertainty in both intensity and damage calculations within this category. The March 2025 NAIC primer describes each simulated event as having expected damage—a mean loss—and uncertainty expressed as a standard deviation. Implementations differ: the 2024 technical paper says some vendors generate a distribution around a mean, while others use different loss values for the same event. Policy terms are then applied in estimating insured loss. [partial-access]

Synthesis: “secondary uncertainty” is unrelated to the primary-peril/secondary-peril terminology used for causal or cascading hazards. The shared adjective does not create a shared classification.

Aleatory and epistemic identify why it is uncertain

A separate vocabulary asks about the source of uncertainty. Aleatory uncertainty is inherent probabilistic variability or randomness. Epistemic uncertainty comes from incomplete information or knowledge and may be reduced as knowledge improves. Oasis LMF states the general randomness-versus-knowledge distinction; a 2014 presentation calls aleatoric uncertainty irreducible probabilistic variability and epistemic uncertainty the kind that more information can reduce. [partial-access]

Keep the two vocabularies separate. Primary does not mean aleatory, and secondary does not mean epistemic. Primary/secondary tells you whether the uncertainty concerns event occurrence or conditional loss. Aleatory/epistemic tells you whether uncertainty reflects randomness or limits in knowledge. The supplied pack provides no one-to-one mapping. Indeed, the 2014 presentation treats both event-frequency and event-severity terms as aleatoric, and the Oasis paper discusses aleatory and epistemic sources within damage modeling. [partial-access]

Synthesis: use two separate questions in review: “What quantity is uncertain?” and “Why are we uncertain about it?” Record both answers without arrows or equivalence signs between the classifications.

A stochastic tail is still conditional on a model

A displayed loss distribution can represent event occurrence and conditional-loss variability while still omitting important uncertainty about the model's structure, assumptions, parameters and data. Synthesis. A smooth EP curve therefore should not be read as a complete confidence statement. The IAA chapter says a well-designed single-model loss distribution represents aleatory uncertainty, says estimating epistemic uncertainty requires multiple models with different parameterizations, and warns that model confidence bands may not be all-inclusive or robust. [partial-access]

Synthesis: multiple-model comparison can expose sensitivity to some model choices, but it samples only the models and parameterizations examined. Detailed interpretation of model spread belongs in Chapter 7. Here the operational point is narrower: uncertainty shown inside one stochastic output and uncertainty about the system that generated it are not the same object.

Synthesis. Sampling variability adds another practical distinction. A finite simulation of annual losses samples event occurrence and conditional severity, so a reported tail estimate can move with the simulated years even when the underlying model assumptions stay fixed. A 2014 presentation reports that, in the platform and setup it studied, sampling error for a 1-in-200 PML could exceed 12%; the excerpt does not state the peril, region or OEP/AEP basis, so the figure should not be generalized. [partial-access]

Synthesis: when presenting a tail result, identify which modeled variations it includes, which knowledge uncertainties were tested outside the run, and whether simulation sampling error was assessed. That makes the limits of the displayed distribution visible without pretending every uncertainty can be collapsed into one band.

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

Primary and secondary describe what is uncertain.

Aleatory and epistemic describe why it is uncertain.

A model's stochastic output does not contain every uncertainty about the model.

07

Totals can match while local losses diverge

Agreement depends on what you compare

Matching portfolio totals does not establish local or tail agreement.

A comparison needs a declared basis

Synthesis. Two model results are comparable only after the practitioner identifies what each number represents: peril, geography, exposure dataset, valuation basis, financial perspective, metric and aggregation level. Similar portfolio averages can coexist with very different location-level patterns, while a difference in totals may arise from exposure or contract inputs rather than hazard or vulnerability science. The March 2025 NAIC primer says results can vary significantly even with the same exposure input because data specifications and assumptions differ; with different exposure inputs, attribution becomes harder still. [partial-access]

Synthesis. Begin by asking whether the comparison holds inputs constant. A same-exposure run can help locate differences in model assumptions, although it does not by itself identify which assumption causes them. A published comparison in which each modeler supplies a distinct exposure dataset measures the combined effect of model and input differences. That result can reveal decision-relevant spread, but it cannot rank intrinsic model quality. [partial-access]

Differences can enter at every module

Hazard estimates become less reliable where events are infrequent and observations are sparse. Exposure uncertainty enters through the quality and completeness of the portfolio description. Vulnerability functions can reflect assumptions used to construct generic damage curves. Financial structures may introduce basis risk when policy or reinsurance terms are represented imperfectly. These are diagnostic categories: they direct investigation to the evidence behind each calculation rather than supplying a formula for allocating the final model spread. [partial-access]

Sources describe the interaction differently. A November 2024 Australian technical paper refers to compounding uncertainty across exposure, vulnerability, frequency and severity, while a 2018 Geneva Association report says the weakest component determines model quality. Neither passage quantifies a combination rule. Model vendors may also interpret historical records and scientific data differently or use different vulnerability and site-condition data. A single loss estimate conceals those choices; even an exceedance-probability curve is better understood as surrounded by uncertainty than as an exact boundary. [partial-access]

One flood study, three distinct comparisons

Synthesis — reading key. The national result compares modeled losses with historical claims; the Florida burn-rate table compares modeled mean loss per unit of coverage; the ZIP-code coefficients compare local spatial patterns. These are different tests of agreement. [partial-access]

A February 2026 study of U.S. catastrophe flood models illustrates why scope must remain explicit. At the national aggregate level, the quoted excerpt reports a 4% differential against historical National Flood Insurance Program claims. The pack’s accompanying summary identifies the models as Verisk, KatRisk and Moody’s RMS. These statements concern national historical-loss representation; they do not establish Florida or ZIP-level agreement. [partial-access]

For the Florida comparison, the study reported burn rate, defined as average annual loss per $1,000 of coverage, for five models: FPFLM $1.81, KCC $0.17, Aon $2.20, KatRisk $2.75 and Verisk $2.26. The authors state that each modeler used a distinct exposure dataset. The study discusses differences in depth-damage functions without apportioning the observed spread among exposure, hazard and vulnerability. [partial-access]

At the Florida ZIP-code level, a third comparison used three of those models: Aon, KCC and FPFLM. Reported pairwise coefficients of determination ranged from 0.10 for Aon–KCC to 0.47 for Aon–FPFLM. This supports the authors' conclusion that models can look similar in aggregate while varying widely at ZIP level. It does not provide a complete five-model correlation matrix, and it should not be merged with the national 4% finding or the five-model Florida burn-rate table. [partial-access]

Synthesis. A disciplined comparison moves from broad alignment to the decision's actual resolution. First reconcile exposure and financial basis. Duties, model acceptance and use review coexist

Know which authority is speaking

Professional duties, model acceptance and insurer-use scrutiny answer different questions.

Separate three kinds of authority

Synthesis. Governance becomes clearer when three questions are kept apart. A professional standard asks what an actuary must understand, consider, document or disclose. A model-acceptance body asks whether a model satisfies criteria for a specified regulatory use. An insurer-use review asks why a company selected, configured and applied a model in a filing or capital process. These forms of oversight can operate at the same time; the supplied sources do not establish a linear shift from certification to use review. [partial-access]

Synthesis. Authority also depends on jurisdiction, date and legal status. A professional paper or technical guide can explain good practice without being legislation. A regulator's primer can describe state approaches without making them uniform. The examples below are historical snapshots reported by sources dated from 2018 through April 2026, not a statement of law or regulatory status in September 2026. [partial-access]

Professional duties travel with the practitioner

A 2018 American Academy of Actuaries monograph cites ASOP No. 38 under the title Using Models Outside the Actuary's Area of Expertise (Property and Casualty) and says actuarial standards guide what an actuary should consider, document and disclose. It also says U.S. standards require at least a basic understanding of a model. A November 2024 Australian technical paper cites a July 2021 title, Catastrophe Modelling (for All Practice Areas), and says professional requirements may require Australian members to assess whether catastrophe models are used appropriately. [partial-access]

Synthesis. The two sources reflect different dates and professional contexts. The supplied excerpts do not provide ASOP 38's full requirements, adoption history or a detailed account of what changed in 2021. Nor is the Australian technical paper itself law. The durable practitioner lesson is narrower: identify the standard that applies to the assignment, then document the understanding and judgement that standard requires. [partial-access]

Model acceptance answers a bounded question

Florida provides a dated example of model-focused review. According to the 2018 Academy monograph, the Florida Commission on Hurricane Loss Projection Methodology was created in the 1995 legislative session to evaluate hurricane models in support of rates that are neither excessive nor inadequate. A 2018 Geneva Association report described the commission as certifying catastrophe models for use in setting homeowners' windstorm insurance rates. That acceptance has a defined peril, purpose and jurisdiction; it is not a universal endorsement of every output or use. [partial-access]

The NAIC materials show a different institutional role. The March 2025 Primer says the NAIC Catastrophe Computer Modeling Handbook had been published in 2010 and was later referred to as the Primer. The supplied March 2025 copy records working-group adoption and says further adoption was pending as of August 13, 2025; the pending adoption level is not resolved by the supplied pack. Its stated purpose is to give state insurance regulators a basic understanding, not to be all-inclusive. [partial-access]

Use review asks what the insurer did

The March 2025 NAIC Primer says state departments of insurance did not take the same approach to insurer use of catastrophe models. Its South Carolina example asks a company to explain why it selected a model. Separately, the Geneva Association reported in 2018 that under Solvency II scrutiny fell on a (re)insurer's use of catastrophe models rather than on the models themselves, including whether the insurer sufficiently understood models used for capital requirements. These are examples from different jurisdictions, not one harmonised regime. [partial-access]

California adds another dated case. The NAIC Primer reported that, as of December 12, 2024, California had passed regulations allowing insurers to use catastrophe models to project fire risk in overall rates. An April 7, 2026 California SB 254 study report said insurers could use wildfire catastrophe models under the Sustainable Insurance Strategy and linked that permission to targets for writing and maintaining policies in wildfire-distressed areas. The same study recommended continued support for state legislation to build a public catastrophe model and noted a March 25, 2026 announcement. These statements describe the sources' reporting at those dates; current requirements require a fresh jurisdiction-specific check. [partial-access]

Read every governance statement with four labels

Synthesis. For any rule, standard or approval, record the authority, jurisdiction, effective or reporting date, and decision in scope. Then ask separately whether the model is accepted, whether the organisation may use it, and what the responsible professional must justify. This prevents a Florida rate-use certification, a professional standard and a European capital-use review from being treated as interchangeable evidence. [partial-access]

09

Selection is complete only when the user can explain, test and govern the result.

Own the basis used in the decision

Model selection, adjustment and validation need an accountable documented rationale.

Accountability is the meaning of ownership

A model may come from a vendor, broker or internal team, but the organization using its output remains responsible for the basis carried into a decision. The IAA Risk Book says the onus remains on the model user to understand, validate and refine assumptions; a Geneva Association report likewise assigns the choice of adjustment path to the user. [partial-access]

Synthesis. In this guide, an organization’s own basis means the selected model configuration, adjustments, assumptions and interpretation that it is prepared to defend for a stated use. It does not mean ownership of vendor software, source code or intellectual property. Nor is “own view of risk” a standardized definition in the supplied pack: the sources use view of risk loosely for a market view, a client view and blended output. [partial-access]

Select for the intended use

Selection begins with fitness for purpose rather than a general ranking of models. A November 2024 Australian-context technical paper asks whether a model adequately represents hazard risk and is sufficiently documented and supported for understanding. It also calls for sensitivity testing of key building attributes, including occupancy, construction class, year built and height. A 2022 Milliman article adds that the depth of vetting depends on the model’s intended use. [partial-access]

The result should be a written rationale connecting those criteria to the actual decision. The NAIC primer reproduces a Maryland questionnaire that asks why one vendor was selected over others. That dated regulatory example is useful because it asks for reasoning, not merely a vendor name. [partial-access]

Synthesis. Model disagreement makes the scope of that rationale important. Chapter 7 shows that agreement at portfolio aggregate can coexist with large local differences. Selection records should therefore identify the portfolio, peril, geography, loss basis, metrics and decision thresholds examined. A statement that two models are “close” is incomplete until it says where and on what measure.

Synthesis — fit gate. Before evaluating accuracy, check that the model covers the relevant perils, lines and territories; represents the portfolio’s financial terms at the resolution the decision needs; and offers calibration evidence, documentation and review that the user can inspect. This is a practitioner checklist derived from the intended-use principle, not a quotation or universal regulatory requirement.

Validate the parts that can change the decision

Validation is a structured challenge, not a single back-test. The Australian paper calls for understanding significant differences in results and supports comparison with historical event data and other vendor results. The Milliman article names reasonability, mapping, sensitivity and outlier analysis as evaluation strategies. [partial-access]

Synthesis. Those tests answer different questions. Historical comparison can reveal calibration problems but is constrained by sparse and changing experience. Mapping checks whether exposure and hazard meet in the right places. Sensitivity testing shows which assumptions move the result. Outlier analysis locates records or areas that may be driving totals. Together, they create evidence for judgment; none certifies that the model is complete. The IAA source warns that model incompleteness leaves the user responsible for understanding and refining assumptions. [partial-access]

Synthesis. Adjustments should follow from identified evidence or a declared policy choice. Record the unadjusted result, the change, its rationale, affected outputs and approver. That makes later model updates reviewable and prevents a calibration choice from becoming an undocumented inherited setting.

Blend only when it serves the purpose

Blending is one available response when several models are acceptable. The Milliman article describes it as especially useful in that circumstance, while warning that it can be difficult for exceedance-probability uses. The Australian paper says weighting assumptions can add to rather than reduce result uncertainty and also states that multiple models are not always necessary. [partial-access]

Synthesis. The supplied pack does not establish that a blend outperforms a well-chosen single model. A blend can broaden the represented views, but weights are additional assumptions that need purpose, evidence and governance. Report the constituent models, versions, common exposure basis, metric-specific method and sensitivity to weights. Do not let an average conceal materially different tails or local patterns.

A practical decision record

Synthesis. A defensible record names the decision and owner; states the peril, territory, exposure date and financial basis; identifies model and version; explains selection criteria; records validation evidence and limitations; documents each adjustment or blend weight; compares the final basis with credible alternatives; and captures approval, effective date and triggers for review. This is a working checklist derived from the accountability, fit-for-purpose and validation themes above, not a rule copied from a regulator. [partial-access]

Synthesis. The aim is traceability. When the model, portfolio or decision changes, the organization can see which judgments still hold, which tests must be repeated and who accepts the residual uncertainty.

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

Own the rationale, not the vendor’s intellectual property.

Validate where results can change the decision.

Treat blending as an option with added assumptions.

Provider, platform, adviser and licence describe different parts of the ecosystem.

Separate the model from its access route

Model providers, platforms, brokers and public initiatives play different roles.

Read the marketplace as dated snapshots

The commercial catastrophe-model market developed around a small number of integrated providers, but the supplied sources do not support calling it an oligopoly or assigning market shares. A platform is not automatically an open model

11

Evaluate the changed task, evidence and failure mode—not the AI label.

Locate AI in the actual workflow

AI applications should be evaluated at the module and task where they change evidence or calculations.

Start with the task that changed

Synthesis. “AI in a catastrophe model” is too broad to assess. Machine learning and imagery may change exposure capture, hazard simulation, vulnerability evidence, post-event damage assessment or computational speed. Each use has a different target, training set, benchmark and consequence when it fails. The relevant question is therefore not whether a model uses AI, but which task it performs and what evidence supports that task.

A commissioned exposure-data study in the supplied pack provides a quantified example. The pack’s summary describes a 2025 Milliman study sponsored by a Casualty Actuarial Society committee that compared machine-learning-derived building footprints with parcel centroids for homes in Hillsborough County, Florida. The quoted excerpt reports that the two points were more than ten metres apart for 68% of homes and that average modeled storm-surge expected loss was over three times higher at the footprint-derived location. The pack’s summary qualifies that loss comparison to cases where the footprint point was more than three feet lower. These are changes in modeled location and modeled loss, not validation against surveys or claims. [partial-access]

Synthesis — mechanism. A parcel’s centre and its actual building can sit at different ground elevations. A lower building location can receive a different modeled water depth and hence a different modeled surge loss even though the building itself is unchanged. This explains the sensitivity; independent location and loss evidence are still needed to establish improved accuracy. [partial-access]

The value is peril and task dependent. The same study says exact structure location is not significantly important for many insured perils and calls for future work comparing the accuracy and benefit of footprint data sources and methods. [partial-access]

Synthesis: an exposure-data review should ask whether the peril is sensitive to the changed coordinate or attribute, whether the derived feature was checked against an independent reference, and whether the loss effect reflects improved truth or merely a different input.

Separate vendor-reported uses from benchmark evidence

Vendors report broader applications, but these statements describe their products rather than independent validation. In a February 2026 article, Moody's said imagery can derive property-level roof condition, vegetation and defensible-space attributes at scale. The pack’s summary places the article’s post-event classification example after the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires. The quoted excerpts describe classifying structures as destroyed, partially damaged or untouched to improve early loss guidance and model validation. [partial-access]

Verisk reported in May 2026 that it used AI to synthesize storm footprints and rainfall events, predict wildfire spread, and extract structural and surrounding-vegetation information. It characterized these techniques as integral to its catastrophe models and said it introduced them selectively alongside established physical and statistical representations. Those are vendor claims about implementation and design intent; the supplied excerpts provide no independent insurer-model benchmark for their accuracy. [partial-access]

Human review can remain part of the workflow. A 2020 Verisk pilot described narrowing imagery into a manageable set of cells for manual review. This supports a human-in-the-loop pattern, but it does not establish current performance or adoption. [partial-access]

Synthesis: record vendor evidence as one of three types: implementation description, internally reported validation, or independently reproduced benchmark. Keep product descriptions, modeled sensitivity results and independent validation distinct. A changed modeled loss is not proof of improved predictive accuracy.

Test rare-event behavior and shifted costs

The 2023 US Government Accountability Office assessment offers useful caution, with an important scope limit: the pack’s summary states that it studied machine learning in natural-hazard forecasting and excluded catastrophe models used for insurer risk management. It found that machine learning can struggle to extrapolate beyond its training data and may perform worse than traditional models for high-intensity hurricanes when rare-event training examples are insufficient. [partial-access]

Speed claims also need a complete denominator. GAO reported faster execution by one weather-model emulator, while noting that emulators first require extensive training data, computing resources and specialist development. That is evidence about a forecasting emulator, not a benchmark for insurer catastrophe-model runtime. [partial-access]

Synthesis: evaluate an AI-enabled step with out-of-sample and extreme-event tests, a relevant non-AI baseline, error analysis by geography and exposure class, and a record of training, review and maintenance costs. Preserve physical constraints or expert review where they are part of the proposed control design, but verify their effect. The supplied pack supports selective augmentation and one forecasting-model replacement example; it does not establish that AI should replace the physical core of insurer catastrophe models.

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

Assess the specific task that AI changes.

Keep vendor descriptions separate from independent benchmarks.

Test rare events, extrapolation and the costs shifted into training and review.

12

A model becomes decision-ready through challenge, documentation and revision.

Make uncertainty usable

A reliable operating process combines model output with explicit limits, challenge and revision.

Treat remedies as controls, not cures

Synthesis. A polished distribution does not remove incomplete exposure data, omitted perils, changing hazard conditions or opaque assumptions. Available responses expose or reduce parts of these problems; the supplied excerpts do not show that they eliminate them.

A 2008 guide reported that data-quality issues contributed up to 45% of the gap between modeled and actual incurred losses in the work it described; the pack does not supply enough context to generalize that percentage across portfolios. It proposed an exposure-data quality framework while noting that no accepted industry standard then existed. A 2026 California study said wildfire insurability decisions were being made with incomplete data and proposed collecting previously inaccessible mitigation and suppression data. [partial-access]

For omitted or non-modeled perils, a 2024 Australian technical paper describes adding an allowance to the natural-perils PML and says a single capture methodology is unlikely. An allowance is a visible management response, not a general model of the omitted risk. [partial-access]

Opacity has a similar trade-off. A 2018 actuarial monograph notes that some core assumptions are proprietary and unavailable to users. A 2018 Geneva Association report calls for transparency to support challenge while recognizing commercial limits around intellectual property. [partial-access]

Keep present-day conditioning separate from future projections

Synthesis. A model calibrated to a historical or recent period represents conditions selected for that version. A future climate scenario projects an explicit future horizon under stated assumptions. They are different analytical products even when both modify hazard frequency or severity.

The Geneva Association reported in 2018 that non-stationarity was a key risk-assessment challenge, that vendors had produced versions calibrated to different historical periods, and that their reliability for placement and risk transfer was debated. [partial-access]

A 2024 Australian technical paper distinguishes short-term variability and exposure changes from longer-term climate effects, and describes climate projections as more complex and uncertain. [partial-access]

Synthesis: label the basis as present-day conditioned risk or a future scenario projection. For present-day conditioning, state the calibration period and intended conditions. For a future projection, state the horizon, scenario, variables changed and treatment of exposure. Do not attribute an observed or modeled loss change to climate change without separate evidence; portfolio growth, location, vulnerability, financial terms and model revision can also change loss.

Challenge AI and conventional models by the same decision standard

Machine learning adds validation and explanation questions. GAO reported in 2023 that no standardized process existed for assessing machine-learning models in its hazard-model scope, and that explainable AI was difficult to incorporate. Its policy options included guidelines for reporting training-data choices, with possible added cost and slower development. [partial-access]

Verisk stated in 2026 that its controls included physics-based guardrails, expert validation, cross-peril checks and documentation. This is vendor-reported, not evidence in the supplied pack of independent effectiveness. [partial-access]

Governance applies beyond AI. The 2024 Australian paper calls for complete, accurate and consistent data, with limitations understood, and warns that adjustments may add to result uncertainty. The Geneva Association described adjustment and blending as viable options, not proof of superior results. [partial-access]

Synthesis — operating review sequence:

  1. Frame the decision. Record peril, portfolio, financial viewpoint, horizon and threshold.
  2. Declare the basis. Identify version, data date, conditioning period or future scenario, and adjustments.
  3. Inspect inputs and omissions. Test exposure quality; list missing data, non-modeled perils and allowances.
  4. Challenge the result. Use relevant experience, scenarios, alternatives or sensitivities; investigate differences.
  5. Review controls. For AI steps, document training scope, rare-event tests, benchmark, human review and explanation limits; record validation evidence and proprietary constraints for every model.
  6. Decide and sign off. State outputs used, fitness for the decision, ownership of residual limits and review triggers.
  7. Monitor and revise. Revisit when exposure, science, versions, contracts or horizon changes.

This authored practitioner synthesis is not a regulatory rule or substitute for professional standards. It creates a traceable chain from decision to evidence, limits and revision.

KEEP THESE THREE IDEAS

Use controls to expose residual limitations rather than declare them solved.

Label present-day conditioning and future scenarios as different analytical bases.

Leave a traceable record of challenge, decision, ownership and revision.

REFERENCE / THE TERMS THAT MATTER

Keep the distinctions close.

Concise working definitions. Read the chapters for assumptions and source differences; labels such as gross and net need a declared basis.

AAL
Mean annual total loss, averaged across modeled years. It is a mean, not a severe-year budget. [partial-access]
OEP / AEP
OEP describes the largest occurrence in a year; AEP describes the sum of occurrences in that year. [partial-access]
Return period
The reciprocal of annual exceedance probability. A probability statement, not a recurrence schedule. [partial-access]
PML / return-period loss
A loss amount at a selected probability. Always state the curve, financial basis, portfolio and time horizon. [partial-access]
TVaR
A tail-average measure. Declare the threshold, loss variable and, for discrete data, how probability at the threshold is handled. [partial-access]
Event catalogue
Simulated event scenarios with characteristics and occurrence probabilities or rates. Event and annual tables serve different aggregation steps. [partial-access]
Exposure
The locations, values and characteristics of the assets at risk, together with relevant policy information. [partial-access]
Vulnerability
The link from local hazard intensity and asset characteristics to expected damage or a damage distribution. [partial-access]
Ground-up / insured
Here, ground-up means damage before policy terms; insured means the modeled payment after policy terms and before reinsurance. Declare the convention in any comparison. [partial-access]
Ceded / retained
Here, ceded is the modeled reinsurance recovery; retained is insured loss less that recovery. This is the guide’s stated teaching convention. [partial-access]
Primary / secondary uncertainty
Event occurrence versus the loss conditional on an event. These terms do not mean primary versus secondary perils. [partial-access]
Aleatory / epistemic uncertainty
Random variability versus uncertainty associated with incomplete knowledge. This pair is not a relabelling of primary/secondary uncertainty. [partial-access]
Burn rate
In the flood study, AAL per US$1,000 of coverage. A coverage-normalized loss measure, with exposure comparability still to be checked. [partial-access]
Calibrated basis / view of risk
In this guide, the documented choices, adjustments and limitations used for the portfolio. Accountability for that basis does not mean ownership of vendor intellectual property. This is a working synthesis. [partial-access]
