Chapter 1 · 4 min read

Why catastrophe models exist

Rare, correlated, severe losses cannot be priced from claims experience, so insurers simulate events instead; the same logic was proposed for pandemic and longevity risk in 2008, but adoption is not documented in these sources.

Where experience stops working

Most property and casualty pricing leans on history. Where losses are "dominated by reasonably predictable and frequent events", actuaries can adjust recent experience for inflation and other changes and project it forward [S10 p.8]. Catastrophes remove that footing: the events are infrequent, so the available history may not predict future loss potential [S10 p.8]. Standard approaches built on historical claims and loss data "are not appropriate for most types of catastrophes", and because the historical data are sparse, any approach carries significant uncertainty [S11 p.4].

Two features compound the difficulty. The events are rare, so the tail is barely observed. And one event produces claims across many contracts at once, so the quantity to estimate is the portfolio's loss from each possible event, not the average policy's loss. Insurers after Andrew wanted "a more scientific approach to estimating catastrophic loss exposure and risk aggregation" [S02 p.4]. A catastrophe model supplies it by simulating events and applying them to the portfolio (chapters 2 and 4).

1992: Andrew forces the change

The first hurricane loss model arrived in the late 1980s, but most insurers did not initially adopt it, even after Hurricane Hugo in September 1989 became the costliest US hurricane to that date [S02 p.4]. Before Andrew, insurers "depended only on historical claims experience" [S12 p.4].

Andrew struck Florida and Louisiana in August 1992 and cost insurers $15.5 billion in nominal dollars, nearly four times Hugo's cost [S02 p.4]. A few hours after landfall, one modeler shared a real-time estimate of $13 billion [S10 p.8]. Insurers "quickly embraced modeling" [S02 p.4]. The NAIC primer says Andrew and Northridge led actuaries to see that probabilistic simulation could estimate probable maximum losses for severe events [S12 p.4], and the IAA Risk Book dates the acceleration of industry adoption to after both [S11 p.4].

Insolvency counts differ: nine after Andrew in one source [S10 p.8], eleven in another, from an A.M. Best count for 1969-2012 [S11 p.4], and only "several" in a third [S12 p.5]. This guide gives the range.

1994 to 2008: later shocks refine the method

Northridge (1994) "marked the end of an approach to assessing earthquake risk in California strictly based on loss experience" [S12 p.5]. The California Earthquake Authority was created in the mid-1990s to help restore the residential insurance market after it [S23 p.2].

Katrina (August 2005) then showed that a model can fail too. It cost insurers more than $41 billion, mostly in New Orleans, and initial model-based damage estimates identified only about one-fourth of that total [S02 p.4]. Models had underestimated coastal flooding driven by wind or storm surge, and often assumed all properties were built to existing codes [S02 p.4]. Hurricane Ike (2008) caused inland damage that far exceeded expectations [S02 p.4]. Marsh's conclusion: "Catastrophe models need constant updating based on experience" [S02 p.4]; the Geneva Association adds that events also exposed model limitations and the importance of understanding underlying assumptions [S14 p.14].

Other shocks widened the list of modelled perils. The loss of the World Trade Center towers on 11 September 2001 prompted terrorism risk models [S03 p.21]. Hurricane and earthquake models came first, followed by severe convective storm, wildfire, flood, terrorism and pandemics [S10 p.39].

The bridge to other practices

The logic that justified models in property has a general form. In 2008, RMS's chief research officer argued that cat modelling was moving into life and casualty, where losses build more slowly but with "the potential for unforeseen correlations in loss across many independent insurance policies and lines of business" [S03 p.21]. "Independent" here means separately written; the losses are dependent through a shared driver. In this guide's terms, a risk is cat-like when a common driver, many contracts and a short history coincide.

Two examples were given. For life and health insurers, "the most critical catastrophic events concern pandemics"; RMS began modelling pandemics in 2004 and built a probabilistic pandemic influenza model in 2006-2007, aimed in part at how costs correlate across an insurer's portfolio [S03 p.21]. For pensions, the article notes that life insurance and pensions had traditionally been "entirely dependent on actuarial analysis" to set premiums and manage reserves [S03 p.22]. For a pension awarded to a 65-year-old male in 2008, one extra year of expected life raises payments by 7 to 8%; rather than trending mortality statistics likely to be at least 20 years out of date, disease progressions and probabilities "can be modelled" [S03 p.22]. That is a proposal, not a described model.

Beyond insurance, the 2008 article argued that the approach could be applied to the financial sector [S03 p.22]; the Geneva Association (2018) reports (re)insurers working with the international community to transfer the technologies to public-sector uses in middle- and low-income countries, such as disaster risk management, development planning and sovereign risk transfer [S14 p.13]; and a 2025 conference deck calls the industry's experience "a valuable playbook" [S24 p.6]. These sources document no adoption of cat-style models in life, health or pensions after 2008.

Caveats. No source gives adoption statistics or Northridge's insured loss. One scanned Milliman article puts Andrew at $30 billion with no stated basis; it is not used [S18 p.1]. The IAA Risk Book excludes pandemics from its scope [S11 p.3].

  2. Treat every model as revisable: Katrina and Ike exposed wrong assumptions, so budget for updating.
  3. Before exporting the method to another practice, test for a common driver, many contracts and a short history, and label what is documented and what is proposed.

The four modules

Every catastrophe model turns events into a loss distribution through hazard, vulnerability and financial calculations on exposure data that enter at every stage; sources count four modules or three, but the hand-offs are the same.

One pipeline, four modules

A catastrophe model turns a portfolio and a catalog of simulated events into a distribution of losses [S12 p.17]. The NAIC primer divides the framework for modeling the impact of natural hazards on a portfolio into four modules: hazard, vulnerability, exposure and financial [S12 p.14]. The Geneva Association also names four key components [S14 p.15], and the IAA Risk Book says the same components apply to all catastrophe types and peril regions [S11 p.4]. Vendor terminology "may vary slightly" [S12 p.14].

Hazard: events, rates and footprints

The hazard module generates event scenarios, determines the path of each, and assesses the local impact as the event progresses in time and space [S12 p.14]. Its event catalog is a probabilistic event set, a database of simulated scenario events, each with a strength or size, location, path and annual probability of occurrence, also called its event rate [S12 p.15]. Each event has its own footprint, the intensity and extent of the hazard over its path [S12 p.15]: for a windstorm, sustained wind speed or peak gusts; for a flood, depth, extent and velocity [S12 p.15].

The hand-off to vulnerability is a local intensity for each event at each location [S12 pp.14-15].

Exposure: what is at risk, and on what terms

The exposure module houses the portfolio data: each building's address or coordinates, its risk characteristics and its insured values, together with policy terms such as deductibles, limits and any reinsurance [S12 p.16]. The Geneva Association defines the module by its relation to the hazard: "The proximity of properties or infrastructure to the hazard is what generates risk" [S14 p.15]. Required detail includes detailed location information, total insured value and exposure characteristics [S12 p.17]. Output uncertainty rises when the input data are inaccurate or have material gaps and rely on assumptions [S12 p.17].

Sources place policy terms differently: inside the exposure module [S12 p.16], or as inputs to the financial module [S17 p.22].

Vulnerability: damage with a mean and a spread

The vulnerability module "calculates the expected damage to the properties at risk, given the hazard intensity, using damage functions" [S12 p.15]. What sets a building's susceptibility is its construction material, occupancy type, year of construction and height [S03 p.7]; the NAIC adds roof age, roof-to-wall connection and opening protection [S12 p.16]. When mitigation features are in the exposure data, most models reflect them through vulnerability curves [S12 p.16], a direct route from exposure into damage.

Damage is quantified as a mean damage ratio, the average anticipated loss over replacement value, with estimates of uncertainty (standard deviations) around it [S03 p.7]. The hand-off to the financial module is a mean damage ratio with its spread, by coverage (structure, contents, time element) [S03 p.7].

Financial: from damage to a loss distribution

The financial module "translates the physical damage calculated in the vulnerability module to provide the dollar amount of financial loss" by applying policy conditions such as deductibles and limits [S12 p.17]. It can report several perspectives: ground up (damage before coverage limits and deductibles), gross (after deductibles, limits and attachment points) and net (after treaty cessions, facultative and so on) [S17 p.21].

The output is a list of simulated events with loss amounts, held as an event loss table or year loss table [S12 p.13]. Because each event carries its probability [S12 p.15], aggregating all event losses yields a loss probability distribution, from which expected losses and the likelihood of different loss levels follow [S12 p.17]. Chapter 4 reads that distribution.

How sources count modules

The count changes with where the cuts fall. The NAIC primer and the Geneva Association give four: hazard, vulnerability, exposure and financial [S12 p.14; S14 p.15]. The 2008 Review guide also has four "modules", but they are stochastic event, hazard, vulnerability and financial analysis, with building inventories as an input rather than a module [S03 p.7]. The IAA Risk Book lists four components: event catalogs, intensity formulas, damage functions and a financial module [S11 p.1]. A 2019 reinsurance bootcamp deck and the Australian technical paper count three: hazard or peril, vulnerability and financial [S17 p.17; S22 p.17]. Under the labels, the same steps recur: simulate events, express local intensity, convert it to damage, apply terms.

Dependencies, not a chain

In this guide's reading, the flow is ordered but not linear. The 2008 hurricane walk-through starts with the user entering location data and building characteristics; the vulnerability step then retrieves hazard intensity and combines it with those characteristics; the financial step uses building values and policy terms [S03 p.2]. The 2019 slides assign inputs to modules the same way [S17 p.22]. The Australian paper has the vulnerability module combine "the event characteristics from the Hazard Module with information relating to the risks exposed" [S22 p.17], and applies coverage features to the damage afterwards [S22 p.4]. Exposure data therefore enter at every stage, so weaknesses in them reach every later step. Each step can add significant uncertainty to gross and net projections [S22 p.4].

Caveats. No source states that the modules form a dependency graph or shows a feedback path; the description is this guide's synthesis of S03 p.2, S12 pp.16-17, S17 p.22 and S22 p.4. The Australian paper is internally mixed: its section 4 lists exposure data, peril modelling and vulnerability, while its annexure counts three modules [S22 pp.3, 17].

  3. Audit exposure data first: it enters every stage, so its errors reach the loss distribution.

Perils and model maturity

Hurricane and earthquake models are the longest established and least variable, younger models cover the frequent perils now setting loss records, and most recent loss growth is attributed to exposure and cost rather than hazard.

A working split, and two other meanings

This guide splits perils into two families. Primary perils are hurricane and earthquake, the first to be modelled and the longest established [S10 p.39; S12 p.26]. Secondary perils are severe convective storm (SCS), wildfire and flood, which are frequent loss drivers with younger models [S12 pp.9, 23]. The split is this guide's convention. No source defines "primary", "peak" or "secondary" peril in this sense; the NAIC primer implies it when it asks for more regular observation of "the losses caused by secondary perils", with SCS as its example [S12 p.9].

Frequency changes the shape of the curve. For very low frequency, high severity perils such as earthquakes, the occurrence and aggregate curves are very similar and typically "converge in the tail"; for frequent perils such as tornado outbreaks, aggregate losses can be significantly higher than the largest single event at every probability level [S11 p.8].

Technical papers use "secondary peril" differently, for hazards triggered by a main event: "storm surge, liquefaction and fire following earthquake" [S22 p.8]. The bootcamp slides add demand surge and say such perils "may contribute substantially to portfolio loss estimate" [S17 p.11]; the Geneva Association and Oasis LMF use the same cascading sense [S14 p.30; S20 p.3]. This guide calls them cascading perils. Neither meaning is secondary uncertainty, "the uncertainty in loss given that an event has occurred" [S22 p.19].

Maturity: older models, tighter results

Hurricane and earthquake models were introduced first, followed by severe convective storm, wildfire, flood, terrorism and pandemics [S10 p.39]. The NAIC primer says these models "have existed longer than wildfire models" and give results that "are more consistent and exhibit less variability than results from less mature models" [S12 p.26]. It calls wildfire models, like flood models, "less mature in their development than other catastrophe models" [S12 p.23]. Milliman says flood and wildfire "have not been the focus of catastrophe modeling for as long" [S18 p.2].

Younger does not mean unreliable: "this does not mean that less mature models are unreliable", though effective use may require more analysis of how results were reached [S12 p.26]. Nor does maturity mean certainty: one 2014 deck reports "50% – 230% of PML estimate of 1-in-100 year US hurricane loss produced by physical models", citing a 2011 Guy Carpenter study [S21 p.2].

Where recent losses sit

SCS is the clearest recent case. Global insured SCS losses for 2023, 2024 and 2025 combined were USD208 billion in today's dollars, USD176 billion of it in the US [S19 p.4]. US insured SCS losses were USD66 billion in 2023, USD59 billion in 2024 and at least USD51 billion in 2025, in today's dollars [S19 p.38]. In 2025 SCS drove at least 47% of insured losses, USD60 billion, and the report calls the peril "a dominant annual loss driver for the industry" [S19 p.4].

Wildfire set its own record: the 2025 US season was the costliest on record for the industry (>USD41 billion), ahead of 2017 and 2018 (USD21 billion each) [S19 p.36]. The US also went without a landfalling hurricane for the first season since 2015, and tropical cyclone losses were significantly reduced [S19 p.36].

For the tail, the nearest evidence is historical. In Gallagher Re's ranking of the costliest events by economic loss, in 2025 dollars, the top 13 are all earthquakes and hurricanes; floods first appear at 14th [S19 p.69].

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires carry two figures: USD65 billion economic in the Gallagher Re report [S19 p.36] and about $53 billion economic (Munich Re) in the SB 254 report [S23 p.28]; the sources and dates differ.

Is it the hazard?

Rising losses are not the same as a rising hazard. Gallagher Re attributes 80-90% of US SCS loss growth since 2000 to non-hazard drivers: expansion of the urbanized footprint, growth in housing units and the cost of construction and labor, with inflation and claims litigation also playing a role [S19 p.38]. The other 10-20% covers changing SCS behavior, any climate influence and ENSO variability [S19 p.38]. The IAA Risk Book likewise ties rising frequency and severity of catastrophe losses over past decades primarily to increasing concentrations of population and property [S11 p.1]. The NAIC primer reports disasters costing over $1 billion rising from an average of three a year in the 1980s to 13 a year in the 2010s [S12 p.9]. Say losses are rising, not the hazard.

Australia: "non-modelled perils"

The Australian technical paper (November 2024) names cyclone, earthquake, bushfire, flood, storm and hail as the main six perils for reinsurance [S22 p.18]. Vendors provide widely used systems for tropical cyclones and earthquakes; bushfire, flood, storm and hail are collectively known as "non-modelled perils" because vendors do not traditionally provide models for them [S22 pp.18-19]. Some vendors now have models for some of them, "but these have not been universally accepted" [S22 p.19]. This is a maturity statement in the paper's own vocabulary, not a "secondary" one.

Caveats. No source defines primary or secondary perils in this sense or ranks perils by contribution to the modelled tail (PML or TVaR); the tail point rests on historical event rankings. S19's publisher is inferred; the 47% share's denominator and the wildfire dollar basis are not stated. S22's Australian context is inferred from its references.

Reading the curve

The exceedance probability curve is the model's product; AAL, OEP, AEP, return period, PML and TVaR are readings of it, and each keeps something and discards something.

The curve is the product

The EP curve is the product; the other metrics are readings of it. The model generates it by running the event catalog against exposures "for each event and year" [S12 p.19], simulating thousands of stochastic events, often across thousands of possible years [S18 p.1]. The IAA Risk Book calls it the primary model output, "a complete probability distribution" that serves many actuarial analyses, including pricing [S11 p.8]. The same logic reaches beyond property: the burn rate, AAL per $1,000 of coverage, "while commonly applied in property and casualty insurance, can also be used in health insurance" [S16 p.3].

Each metric answers a different question of the same curve: how likely is a loss above X, what loss goes with probability p, what is the mean, what is the average beyond a point. Because AAL and the tail metrics share one curve, a flaw in the curve reaches all of them [S16 p.3].

OEP and AEP

Models typically generate two curves [S11 p.8]. The occurrence curve (OEP) "is derived from the single largest occurrence in a year" [S22 p.19]; the aggregate curve (AEP) is derived "from the sum of losses from all events in a year" [S22 p.17]. The NAIC primer words the same split as the likelihood that a single event, or the year's total, will exceed a specified amount [S12 p.19]. Because a year's total is never smaller than its largest event, "the AEP is always larger than or equal to the OEP at a given return period" [S22 p.19]. AEP is also "more challenging to estimate and less robust than the OEP distribution" [S11 p.8]. Chapter 3 shows how the gap depends on peril frequency.

Return period

The return period "is simply the reciprocal of the exceedance probability" [S12 p.19]: a 1% annual exceedance probability is a 100-year return period, and 0.4% corresponds to a 250-year return period loss [S14 p.45]. It is an average, not a schedule: a 100-year event "could happen in consecutive years or not at all for many centuries" [S12 p.19].

AAL: the mean, and the pricing metric

The average annual loss (AAL) is "the expected value occurring in any given year" and "the most common metric used in catastrophe ratemaking and pricing" [S12 p.19]; it is "simply the average of all the simulated iterations" [S12 p.19] and equals the mean of the EP distribution [S14 p.45]. Theoretically it "should be calculated from the AEP" [S11 p.8], and the result is mathematically the same whether it comes from aggregate exceedance probabilities or event tables [S16 p.3]. Unlike a PML, it adds across risks: "AALs are additive, PMLs are not" [S10 p.31].

PML: an amount at a chosen probability

A probable maximum loss is a loss amount read at a chosen exceedance probability. The AAA monograph says a PML, also called a Return Period Loss, "gives two pieces of information—an amount and a probability" [S10 p.15]. Common points: exceedance probabilities of .01 and .004 in the US, "also referred to as the 100 and 250 year Probable Maximum Losses", and .005 in Europe [S11 p.8]; the Australian paper reads a one-in-200 PML as "the 0.5% (1/200) loss estimate from the OEP curve" [S22 p.19]. Insurers use the return period of a loss size to help set reinsurance attachment and exhaustion levels [S12 p.20].

The basis of a PML is worded three ways: a single event, with the NAIC paragraph defining it both as a probability and as a loss level [S12 p.20]; "by an event or in a year" [S10 p.15]; the OEP curve [S22 p.19]. This guide's rule: a PML is a loss amount at a stated exceedance probability on a named curve.

TVaR: the average beyond the point

A PML is one point and says nothing about what lies beyond it; the Australian paper suggests checking whether the PML flattens out after 200 years or rises sharply [S22 p.13]. TVaR (also called CTE [S11 p.14] or TCE) is "the conditional expectation of losses that are greater than or equal to a specified return period loss" [S17 p.39]. The IAA Risk Book says insurers are "moving to metrics capturing more of the EP curve such as TVaR", measures that "take into account the thickness of the tail" [S11 p.14].

What each reading keeps and discards

AAL keeps the mean of the whole distribution and drops the tail: the AAA monograph's example has portfolios with "similar AALs but different 250-year PML/AAL ratios" [S10 p.26]. A PML keeps one point on a named curve and drops everything beyond it. TVaR keeps the average beyond the threshold and drops the shape of the tail. The AEP keeps the year's total and drops which events made it; the OEP keeps the largest event and drops the rest of the year. Additivity differs too: "The values on the AEP and OEP curves are not additive" [S17 p.27], and neither are PMLs [S10 p.31], because two books' bad years need not coincide.

Caveats. The NAIC primers do not define TVaR; it comes from S10, S11 and S17. Names vary (exceedance or exceeding probability; average annual or annual average loss; CTE or TCE). The keeps-and-discards reading is this guide's synthesis. Beyond property and casualty, the curve's use rests on S11 p.8 and S16 p.3 only.

Exhibits

Takeaway

  1. Treat the EP curve as the product and every metric as a reading of it; state the curve (OEP or AEP) and the basis with every number.
  2. Quote a PML as a loss amount at a stated exceedance probability on a named curve, never as a bare "1-in-100".
  3. Use AAL to add and price, a PML to size one point, and TVaR to see the tail beyond it; do not add PMLs.

Financial terms and decisions

Policy terms and reinsurance turn ground-up loss into gross and net loss, and each decision reads a different, partly overlapping part of the resulting distribution.

One loss, three views

The financial module turns damage into money owed and reports it from three perspectives: ground-up is "damage prior to coverage limits and deductibles", gross is "loss after deductibles, limits, attachment points", and net is "loss after treaty cessions, facultative, etc." [S17 p.21]. Policy terms include deductibles by coverage, aggregate deductibles, total and sub limits, coinsurance and attachment points, and they are applied to each event's loss distribution, not only its mean [S11 p.7]. The terms act on the damage to each insured property [S10 p.14].

This is a transformation, not a scaling. A deductible removes small losses, a limit caps large ones, and reinsurance removes a band in between. Each step can add uncertainty, so "gross and net loss projections can involve very significant uncertainty" [S22 p.4].

A layer, worked through

An occurrence layer pays the slice of each event loss between an attachment point and an exhaustion point; return periods help set both [S12 p.20].

Illustrative (not from the sources): a $100m layer above $50m pays nothing on a $40m event, $40m on a $90m event, and its full $100m on any event of $150m or more. Multiply each event's ceded amount by its annual rate and add the products, and you have the layer's expected annual loss (AAL = Σ rate × loss [S17 p.43]). In the seven-event set of Exhibit E5.1, events above $50m occur 0.107 times a year, events of $150m or more occur 0.012 times a year, and the layer's AAL is $3.95m.

The occurrence exceedance probability (OEP) curve shows "how likely a loss will be ceded to a per occurrence reinsurance layer" [S22 p.19]; read at the exhaustion point, it shows the chance the layer is used up. The AAA monograph's hypothetical hurricane-wind example places a layer between the 100- and 250-year PMLs ($1,315 million and $1,902 million): it is reached with probability 1.0 percent and used entirely with 0.4 percent [S10 pp.30-31]. Reinsurance pricing discussions "often begin with the AAL plus a factor times the standard deviation for the layer" [S10 p.31].

An annual aggregate cover reads a different curve. The aggregate exceedance probability (AEP) "is derived from the sum of losses from all events in a year", so it serves aggregate (stop-loss) covers and reinstatement analysis [S22 p.17]; it is never below the OEP at the same return period [S22 p.19]. The sources mention reinstatements without describing their mechanics.

The 2008 guide says its underwriter "adds 20% of the CV", but its arithmetic, (1.15* $24,000) + (20%* $135,000) = $54,600, applies 20% to the standard deviation ($135,000), not the CV (5.6); read it as 20% of the standard deviation [S03 p.9].

Which decision reads which part of the curve

Pricing uses the average: "Premium = AAL + Expense Load + Risk Load" [S10 p.16], with the AEP supplying the distribution of annualised losses behind catastrophe loads [S22 p.17]. Insurers have "moved from using expected losses and standard deviation metrics" toward pricing on the capital a risk consumes [S11 p.14].

Underwriting and accumulation use marginal effects: "how adding or removing a property affects PML for a book of business", plus a TVaR check on the tail [S10 p.27]. "Although AALs are additive, PMLs are not" [S10 p.31]: in the AAA's hypothetical table the 100-year PMLs by cause sum to $1,651 million (computed here), against $1,458 million for all causes combined [S10 p.31].

Reinsurance purchase reads OEP for per-occurrence layers and AEP for aggregate covers, "used in both purchasing and pricing catastrophe risk reinsurance" [S22 p.19].

Capital and oversight read fixed tail points. US insurers, rating agencies and regulators "have come to rely most heavily on the .01 and .004 loss exceedance probabilities", the 100- and 250-year PMLs, which "are used to set capital requirements, determine how much reinsurance to buy, and for formal risk tolerance statements" [S11 p.14]. The NAIC's catastrophe risk charge uses "100-year PML catastrophe model outputs" for earthquake and hurricane [S12 p.25]; in Australia the return period for the natural-perils capital requirement and for reviewing reinsurance is "often 200 years" [S22 p.13]. Rating agencies "rely on catastrophe models to aid in their assessment of an insurer's solvency risk" [S12 p.13], but only the US statement above names outputs. Because one tail point serves several users, the readings overlap rather than partition.

ILS readers meet the model in the trigger. A modelled-loss transaction runs a modelled event through a notional portfolio and uses the resulting loss estimate as the trigger; "In essence, modelled loss is a more complex and less transparent version of a parametric structure" [S03 p.20]. Model output also gives financial markets "quantitative information" for developing catastrophe bonds [S10 p.32].

Public policy reads the same curve. California's SB 254 study (April 2026) sized a wildfire backstop "attaching at a 1-in-10-year modeled loss level, currently estimated to be about $4 billion"; at the 1-in-100-year modeled loss (about $25 billion) utilities would self-insure below $4 billion and the backstop would cover "the remaining $21 billion" [S23 p.98].

Caveats. Capital and rating-agency use beyond property/casualty is not in the sources, and no source maps AAL, OEP, AEP and TVaR one-to-one to these users; the map is compiled from S10, S11, S12 and S22. Post-2018 TVaR practice is not covered. The SB 254 figures are preliminary, basis not stated. The layer example is illustrative.

Two vocabularies of uncertainty

Cat-model layers (which events occur, what each costs) and actuarial kinds (aleatory, epistemic) answer different questions, so neither pair maps onto the other.

Layers: what is uncertain

Cat-model vocabulary splits a loss estimate into two layers. Primary uncertainty is uncertainty "with respect to the events themselves" [S11 p.7]; the bootcamp slides put it as "Uncertainty in the likelihood that a particular event occurs." [S17 p.39]. Secondary uncertainty is "uncertainty in the amount of loss, given that a certain event has occurred" [S17 p.44]. The IAA says it "includes the uncertainty in the intensity and damage calculations" [S11 p.7], and the Australian paper defines only this term: "This relates to the uncertainty in loss given that an event has occurred." [S22 p.19]. The Australian paper attributes that variation to the hazard, the vulnerability of buildings, and the quality and detail of the risk data [S22 p.10]. The word is not "secondary peril", a different idea (Chapter 3).

Kinds: why it is uncertain

The actuarial pair classifies by cause. "In general uncertainty can be characterized as either aleatory or epistemic" [S20 p.2]. Aleatory uncertainty "represents the randomness associated with the process", and epistemic uncertainty is "the uncertainty due to lack of information or knowledge" [S20 p.2]. Krvavych, who spells the first "aleatoric", calls it "irreducible uncertainty representing pure probabilistic variability" and calls the second the kind that "can be reduced by gaining more information" [S21 p.6]. The IAA applies the pair to cat models: aleatory is "inherent process uncertainty"; epistemic is "introduced by the incomplete knowledge of the process parameters and/or the proper model structure" [S11 p.11]. In theory, "aleatory uncertainty is impossible to reduce due to the stochastic nature of the process" [S11 p.11].

Why the pairs do not line up

It is tempting to say primary is aleatory and secondary is epistemic. No source says so. Krvavych treats both terms of the event-loss table, event frequency (λ) and event severity (X), as aleatoric: "first (λ) and second (X) aleatoric uncertainty" [S21 p.13]. Oasis LMF finds both kinds inside the vulnerability module alone, tagging the quality and quantity of claims data as aleatory and the choice of damage-curve method as epistemic [S20 p.2]. The event layer has knowledge gaps too: where events are infrequent, "there will be less reliable data and higher uncertainty" [S11 p.9]. One pair asks what is uncertain, the other why, so both kinds can sit in both layers.

Oasis LMF files data quality and quantity under "aleatory", yet Krvavych defines epistemic as what "can be reduced by gaining more information" [S20 p.2; S21 p.6]; the papers draw the line differently, another reason not to build a lookup between the pairs.

How each layer is carried

Primary uncertainty travels as event rates in a sampled catalog. Each event-loss-table row carries "RATE: Rate of occurrence of each event" [S17 p.43], and catalogs "typically include a large sample of events generated by Monte Carlo simulation or stratified sampling techniques" [S11 p.5]. Simulation first draws an event, then a severity for it, and the sampling itself adds error: a 2014 study found that on a standard platform the "Sampling error of 1-in-200 Probable Maximum Loss (PML) could exceed 12%" [S21 p.13].

Secondary uncertainty travels as a distribution per event. The NAIC primer describes each simulated event as having "expected damage, which is the mean loss, and uncertainty, which is the standard deviation, around the damage estimate" [S12 p.13]. Practitioners often express that spread "in terms of a standard deviation or a coefficient of variation (CV)" [S10 p.14]. Vendors differ. In 2019 bootcamp slides an RMS-style event loss table carried a mean plus independent and correlated standard deviations and assumed a Beta distribution for each event, while an AIR-style table listed mean losses and "Standard deviation is not provided by default in AIR" [S17 pp.43-45]. Approaches "vary by model vendor, with some generating a distribution around a mean loss, and others having different loss values for the same event" [S22 p.10]. The financial module then applies policy terms to these distributions [S11 p.7].

Disagreement is evidence, not a measure

One model's output shows aleatory uncertainty at best: "in a well designed model it is suitably represented by the probabilistic distribution of loss from a single model", whereas "Estimation of epistemic uncertainty requires use of multiple models built using different parameterizations of the natural hazard process" [S11 p.11]. Disagreement between models is therefore evidence of epistemic uncertainty. It is not a full measure: confidence bands from a model "will not be all-inclusive or robust" [S11 p.11], and a comparison covers only the models that happen to exist. The Australian paper is measured: results from several models "may help to understand the uncertainty surrounding the models", yet "it is not necessary to obtain results from multiple models" for an informed view [S22 p.10].

Caveats. No source maps primary to aleatory or secondary to epistemic, and none states the evidence-not-a-full-measure point directly; both are this guide's synthesis of S11 p.11 and S21 p.13. Vendor table detail is from 2019 and the 12% figure is one 2014 study, basis not stated. Framing beyond natural-catastrophe P/C rests on aleatory/epistemic only.

Exhibits

Takeaway

  1. Ask two separate questions of any uncertainty statement: what is uncertain (layer) and why (kind); do not convert one answer into the other.
  2. Know how your model carries each layer: event rates for primary, per-event mean and spread for secondary, and whether your vendor supplies a spread at all.
  3. Treat model disagreement as evidence of epistemic uncertainty, never as its full measure.

Where uncertainty enters, and how models disagree

Uncertainty enters at every module, models built on similar inputs can still disagree widely, and agreement in aggregate does not imply agreement in detail.

Where each module adds uncertainty

Each module contributes its own uncertainty. Hazard: where events are infrequent, "there will be less reliable data and higher uncertainty" [S11 p.9]. Exposure: "The quality of exposure data used in catastrophe modelling is a very important source of extrinsic uncertainty." [S20 p.3]. Vulnerability: generic construction and occupancy damage curves involve "uncertainty due to assumptions made by the model developer" [S20 p.4]. Financial: applying "insurance or reinsurance structures to model results" may add uncertainty through "any related basis risks" [S22 p.9]. Even where loss data exist, as in Florida and the Gulf states, most damage functions rest on expert judgment because there are thousands of them [S11 p.9].

Vendors then diverge on similar inputs. The models are largely built on the same scientific information, and "model differences result from how the data is interpreted and analyzed to develop the many model assumptions" [S11 p.7]. For many peril regions data are sparse, so "model assumptions rely in large part on expert opinion and judgment", and "Model variability and volatility frequently arise from differing scientific opinions and perspectives" [S11 p.11]. Vendors differ in their "Interpretation of the historical record", "Interpretation of detailed scientific data", "Sources of vulnerability" and "Site conditions data" [S17 p.36], and results "can vary significantly, even with the same exposure data input" [S12 p.13]. Even one model's results "can change dramatically with an update to a new version" [S12 p.26].

Compounding or weakest link?

The sources word the combination two ways. The Australian paper sees "compounding uncertainty across modules relating to exposure, vulnerability and both the frequency and severity of peril components", and adds that tail outputs can "further increase the impact of underlying uncertainty" [S22 p.9]. The Geneva Association says the "weakest component of the model determines the quality" of the risk estimate [S14 p.26]. Read literally, the first implies that uncertainty builds as results pass through the modules; the second implies that one poor module caps the quality of the whole. The Australian paper adds that different sources of uncertainty may matter more for smaller or larger loss events [S22 p.9].

"Compounding" (Australian technical paper, 2024) [S22 p.9] and "weakest component" (Geneva Association, 2018) [S14 p.26] are different rules for combining module uncertainty, and the Geneva Association adds that model uncertainty "continues to remain largely unquantified" [S14 p.26]; this guide adopts neither.

One study, three comparisons

A February 2026 study (Dusseau, Zobel and Schwalm, Woodwell Climate Research Center) compares seven US flood models [S16 p.1]. Its three comparisons involve different models, exposure data and bases, so keep them apart. The study also reports First Street's model "roughly twice as high across the United States" [S16 p.1]; that sits outside the three comparisons below.

National, against NFIP claims. The three models used in National Flood Insurance Program pricing (Verisk, KatRisk and Moody's RMS) "accurately represent historical flood losses with a 4% differential compared to NFIP claims data" [S16 p.1]. The mean modelled burn rate is US$1.80 per US$1,000 of coverage against a historical NFIP loss rate of US$1.74 [S16 p.7]. The comparison sets NFIP claims from 1978 to 2024, loss-trended to present-day exposure, against modelled AAL up to the 150-year event [S16 p.8], and the authors caution that the history "is a single sample of a distribution" [S16 p.12].

Florida, model to model. Five models report burn rates, "AAL per US$1,000 of coverage" [S16 p.9]: $1.81 (FPFLM), $0.17 (KCC, Karen Clark & Company), $2.20 (Aon), $2.75 (KatRisk) and $2.26 (Verisk), each on a distinct exposure dataset [S16 p.11]. KCC is "13x lower than Verisk" [S16 p.12]. The authors judge the comparison valid because every dataset derives from NFIP policy data and burn rates are normalised to each dataset's exposure [S16 p.11]. They name two candidate causes for the KCC gap: the depth-damage functions each model uses, and KCC's different method for storm surge, which "likely contributes to the difference in loss rates" [S16 p.12]. The paper does not apportion the gap between them.

Local, zip code. Across three of those Florida models (Aon, KCC, FPFLM), zip-code r-squared runs from 0.10 (Aon and KCC) to 0.47 (Aon and FPFLM) [S16 p.13]. Good agreement at state and national level "does not signify that loss rates are similar across models or accurate at higher spatial resolutions" [S16 p.13]. Milliman's 2022 article says the same of mature models: aggregate results "in the same ballpark", individual risks "may still vary widely" [S18 p.2].

The fuzzy area

No single output shows this spread. The IAA's image is that EP curves are better thought of as “fuzzy areas” versus lines [S11 p.9]. The AAA cautions that "A large range of output does not mean that any model is inaccurate or unreliable" [S10 p.38]. Each comparison above measures a different width of that area, which is why they should not be stacked into one number.

Caveats. The spread evidence is US flood; hurricane wind appears only second-hand (Weinkle and Pielke 2017: five models, 95% band $33 billion to $192 billion for the 100-year event, basis not stated) [S16 p.2]. No source apportions spread to modules or quantifies how they combine. Table 1 excludes RMS, which supplies only storm-surge AAL [S16 p.8].

Takeaway

  1. Trace uncertainty module by module, but do not assume the sources tell you how it combines.
  2. Judge agreement at the resolution you use: agreement in national or portfolio totals says little about a state, a zip code or a single risk.
  3. Keep each comparison's population, exposure data and basis attached to its number.

Standards and regulation

The professional duty to understand and document model use travels with the actuary, while the rules that certify models or scrutinise their use are set jurisdiction by jurisdiction, and both regimes coexist.

The duty: professional standards

The duty follows the actuary; the rulebook follows the jurisdiction. In the US, the Actuarial Standards of Practice "provide guidance for what an actuary should consider, document, and disclose when performing an actuarial assignment" [S10 p.10]. The July 2018 monograph names ASOP No. 38 as "Using Models Outside the Actuary’s Area of Expertise (Property and Casualty)" [S10 p.10]; by 2024 the Australian paper lists the revised standard as "Catastrophe Modelling (for All Practice Areas), July 2021" [S22 p.16]. Model users face a real task to "develop even a basic understanding of the model, as required by U.S. actuaries’ standards of practice" [S10 p.38].

ASOP No. 38 carries different titles by source date, "Using Models Outside the Actuary’s Area of Expertise (Property and Casualty)" in 2018 and "Catastrophe Modelling (for All Practice Areas), July 2021" in 2024 [S10 p.10; S22 p.16]; this guide uses the later title and does not date the standard.

In Australia, the Actuaries Institute's technical paper (November 2024) says the listed standards "may require Members working in Australia to assess that catastrophe models are being used appropriately" [S22 p.3]. It adds that "In most cases, actuaries will need to rely on the work of others" [S22 p.3], and that how far to understand a model should be "consistent with its intended use and the materiality of its outputs" [S22 p.4]. The paper is guidance: it "does not override the requirements in any Professional Standards" [S22 p.2].

The rulebook: jurisdictions

Florida. "In the 1995 Florida Legislative session, the Florida Commission on Hurricane Loss Projection Methodology (FCHLPM) was created to evaluate hurricane models" [S10 p.10], on the premise that reliable projections are necessary "to assure rates are neither excessive nor inadequate" [S10 p.10]. A residential rate filing that relies on a hurricane model is limited to models the Commission has found acceptable [S10 p.10]; the Geneva Association says it "certifies Cat models for use in setting homeowner’s windstorm insurance rates" [S14 p.27]. The Commission also evaluates flood models, though flood filings are informational [S12 p.27].

NAIC. The Catastrophe Computer Modeling Handbook, published in 2010, is "now referred to as the Catastrophe Modeling Primer" [S12 p.20]. In 2017 the NAIC added a specific hurricane and earthquake catastrophe charge to risk-based capital [S12 p.25], which draws on "100-year PML catastrophe model outputs" [S12 p.25]. The March 2025 Primer, written for state regulators who "need a basic understanding of catastrophe modeling" [S12 p.4], was adopted by two NAIC working groups on 25 March 2025, and its text still shows further adoption pending on 13 August 2025 [S12 p.1].

Course slides list a "Wildfire charge effective 12/31/23" [S08 p.19], while the primer says wildfire and severe convective storm models were adopted "for the 2023 and 2024 year-end informational reporting" [S12 p.25]; follow the primer.

States. "State DOIs do not take the same approach to an insurer's use of catastrophe models." [S12 p.25]. South Carolina asks an insurer using an unapproved hurricane model for "An explanation of why the company is using the selected model." [S12 p.28]; Maryland's questionnaire asks it to "Provide the reason you chose this vendor(s) over the other vendors on the list" [S12 p.32].

Europe. Under Solvency II, "it is the use of Cat models by (re)insurers, which is under regulatory scrutiny rather than the models themselves", and insurers must "demonstrate sufficient understanding of the Cat models they use for capital requirements" [S14 p.27]. Other countries, including Peru and Mexico, have introduced model approval processes [S14 p.27].

California. "As of December 12, 2024, California passed regulations that allow insurers to use catastrophe models to project fire risk" reflected in the overall rate level, although the state "has always accepted modeled wildfire losses in the determination of rate segmentation" [S12 p.27]. Under the Sustainable Insurance Strategy (SIS), "insurers can now use wildfire catastrophe models", but those who want to use them "must commit to a target to write and maintain policies in wildfire-distressed areas" [S23 p.69]. The SB 254 study report (7 April 2026) offers options to codify the SIS, which was implemented through rulemaking and bulletins "with certain aspects being challenged in court" [S23 p.70], and to "Continue support for State legislation to build a public catastrophe model" under SB 429, whose call for expertise was issued on 25 March 2026 [S23 p.53].

Both regimes coexist

Florida certifies models; South Carolina and Maryland ask insurers to justify their choices; Solvency II scrutinises use; California ties model use to writing commitments. Nothing in the sources dates a shift from one regime to the other; they stand side by side [S10 p.10; S12 p.28; S14 p.27; S23 p.69]. The professional duty applies under either.

Caveats. This chapter's sources run to April 2026; check for changes. The Primer's final adoption is not confirmed in the text [S12 p.1]. The sources give neither ASOP 38's adoption date nor its requirements beyond "consider, document, and disclose", and no date for the SIS. Applying ASOP 38 beyond property/casualty is the guide's reading of its title.

Takeaway

  1. Keep the duty and the rulebook apart: your standard travels with you, a regulator's rule stops at its border.
  2. Expect two questions, whether the model is acceptable and whether you can justify your use of it, and document the answer to the second.
  3. Date-stamp every regulatory statement, because this chapter's sources end in April 2026.

Choosing and owning a model

Choosing a catastrophe model is a documented process of fitting, testing and, where needed, blending models, and what results is a basis the insurer owns, not the vendors' average view.

Catastrophe model results can vary significantly even on the same exposure data [S12 p.13], so a model cannot simply be taken off the shelf. Choosing one is a documented process: fit it to the job, test it against evidence, then decide what to adjust or combine. The end product is a basis the insurer owns, whichever vendor built the engine.

Choose: fit before features

The Australian actuaries' technical paper (November 2024) treats selection as a fit check. Does the model cover the hazard, perils, lines and regions in the portfolio? Do its financial terms and granularity match the policies? How was it calibrated, is it "sufficiently documented and supported" to be understood, and has it had expert review? [S22 pp.6–7]

Milliman adds a proportion rule: "The level of vetting needed depends on the intended usage of the model" [S18 p.3]. A model built for one purpose may give unreasonable results for another, so new users need particular care [S18 p.3].

Validate against something

Validation needs a benchmark. The Australian paper points to history, other vendors' results and scenarios, and says "Attempts should be made to understand any significant differences in model results" [S22 pp.7–8]. It also asks users to test and document how modelled loss responds to key parameters such as occupancy type and construction class [S22 p.7].

Milliman names four practical tests: "reasonability, mapping, sensitivity, and outlier analysis" [S18 p.3]. Reasonability asks whether results make sense; a storm-surge model whose damage rises with distance from the coast needs investigation, and many zero-dollar results may signal a systemic underestimate [S18 p.3]. Mapping plots results spatially and works best across two or more models [S18 p.3]. Sensitivity asks whether output responds to a driver, such as distance to coast, as it should [S18 p.5]. Outlier analysis needs three or more models and a stated rule, for example one model's gap to the highest of the others [S18 p.5].

In a February 2026 US flood comparison, three models used in NFIP pricing matched historical NFIP claims within a 4% differential [S16 p.1], yet one Florida model's burn rate (average annual loss per US$1,000 of coverage) was 13x lower than Verisk's [S16 pp.9, 12].

Regulators ask for the reasoning as well as the numbers. The Maryland questionnaire reproduced in the NAIC primer asks filers to "Provide the reason you chose this vendor(s) over the other vendors on the list" [S12 p.32]. The Geneva Association observes that the use of models by (re)insurers is under "regulatory scrutiny rather than the models themselves" [S14 p.27].

Blend, with care

Blending weights several models into one estimate. Milliman finds it "especially useful when more than one model appears acceptable" [S18 p.5]. Averaging AALs is simple; blending exceedance probabilities is harder, and a blend is still limited if one model's outliers noticeably move the average [S18 p.5]. Some blending approaches, severity blending for example, lose event loss table information [S21 p.19].

The weights are themselves assumptions. Weighting two models produces "a new model with additional input parameters" [S22 p.9].

Are several models needed? The Australian paper says "it is not necessary to obtain results from multiple models" to form an informed view [S22 p.10]; Milliman says "Employing one catastrophe model might not always be enough" [S18 p.5]. Both are defensible; purpose decides.

Does blending reduce uncertainty? PwC's 2014 slide says independent imperfections can diversify away, so blending "may lead to reduction in uncertainty" [S21 p.19]; the Australian paper says the weights "may add to, rather than reduce, result uncertainty" [S22 p.9].

Owning the view of risk

Standard vendor models are calibrated to meet "the average market view of risk" [S14 p.27]. An individual book is rarely average, so "nearly all users have to recalibrate and adjust model parameters and results" to fit their own risk profiles [S14 p.27]. The NAIC primer describes the same motive: insurers choose model settings that suit their books, adjust modelled output, or combine models [S12 p.13].

Handling model uncertainty by adjustment or blending is a choice that "is the responsibility of the model user" [S14 p.26]. The IAA is blunter: "the onus will remain on the model user to fully understand, validate, and refine" the assumptions [S11 p.12]. Some users lean on advisers; a 2019 boot-camp slide describes a model-suitability service "helping clients formulate their view of catastrophe risk" [S17 p.37].

"View of risk" is a loose phrase. The sources use it for the vendor average [S14 p.27], for the "more customised views of risk" that in-house models offer [S14 p.19], for adjustable models reflecting "different views of risk" [S14 p.20], for a blended output [S11 p.11] and for what different models each show [S18 p.5]. None defines it. This guide's working definition, which is not from the sources: an insurer's view of risk is the documented, calibrated basis for its own portfolio, meaning the model choices, adjustments, blend weights and reasons behind the loss numbers it relies on.

Caveats. the sources give no definition of "view of risk" and no test showing a blend beats a single model. Disagreement figures are US flood only [S16]. Solvency II and Maryland are jurisdiction-specific, the 2019 slide's issuer is unverified [S17], and the framing is property/casualty catastrophe models.

Exhibits

Takeaway

  1. Choose by intended use, and scale the vetting to the use.
  2. Validate against history, other vendors and scenarios, and be ready to explain the choice to a regulator.
  3. Vendors calibrate to the market average; the calibrated, documented basis is the user's to own.

The market and access

A few commercial vendors long led catastrophe modelling; open platforms, vendor-opened platforms, specialist, broker-built and public models now sit beside them, and access runs through licences, consultancies, brokers and public models.

Read this chapter as dated snapshots, not a market census. The sources describe the landscape in 2015, 2018 and 2026.

Three vendors, each with a platform (the 2018 view)

In 2018 the Geneva Association described "The three Cat modelling companies, formed in the 1980s" as AIR Worldwide, RMS and CoreLogic, which "remain the largest" commercial providers [S14 p.19]. Each had "developed their own software platform" as an integrated package, so a user of a vendor's model also needed "the equivalent software platform" [S14 p.25]. That coupling explains the later pressure for "standards and greater interoperability across models" [S14 p.25].

Which three firms? A 2015 Marsh piece on hurricane models names AIR Worldwide, EQECAT and RMS [S02 p.5]; the 2018 report names CoreLogic where that list has EQECAT [S14 p.19]; no source explains the change, and a 2026 California report still names AIR Worldwide (Verisk), CoreLogic and Moody's RMS as licensors behind Aon's output [S23 p.127].

Who else builds models

Around the largest vendors, other builders emerged. Smaller enterprises "specialising by either geography or peril" include JBA, which offers flood models globally plus a growing set of windstorm and cyclone models, and KatRisk [S14 p.19]. Large insurers and reinsurers built their own models for "more customised views of risk", and reinsurance brokers such as "Aon Benfield, Guy Carpenter and Willis Towers Watson" develop models for regions vendors do not cover or to reflect a client's specifics [S14 p.19]. A 2025 conference slide places "Model customization, climate risk inclusion" in the 2010s [S24 p.3]. The mix persists: a February 2026 comparison of US flood models covered "seven catastrophe flood models", including Aon's and the Florida Public Flood Loss Model [S16 p.1].

Platforms open up

An open platform separates the model from the software that runs it. The industry asked for platforms that take internally developed models and run several vendors' models side by side [S14 p.20]. The Oasis Loss Modelling Framework, a non-profit open-source platform funded by the (re)insurance industry, "was launched in 2012" [S14 p.20]. It gives access to models from "multiple providers using a consistent set of standards", and its plug-and-play engine lets developers focus on hazard or vulnerability "without having to re-invent a financial modelling engine" [S14 p.20]. Impact Forecasting has worked with Oasis since its inception, "by putting IF models into the Oasis platform" [S20 p.5]. The platform also gave users access to new models from emerging smaller enterprises, and the Global Earthquake Model initiative planned to distribute its earthquake models to (re)insurers through the Oasis and AIR platforms later in 2018 [S14 p.20].

Vendors opened their own platforms too. AIR began supporting "plug and play of models in their reinsurance platform CATRADER in 2013" and planned to integrate other vendors' and users' models in Touchstone and Touchstone Re "from 2018 onwards" [S14 p.20]. Verisk's 2026 blog describes an "open gateway approach" that "allows integration across Verisk and client ecosystems", including clients' own models [S07 p.3].

Replacement or coexistence? The 2018 report says "There is a place for both commercial, proprietary" tools and open, interoperable frameworks [S14 p.26]; the 2026 licence line above still names the same licensors [S23 p.127]. Neither shows one model displacing the other.

Getting access

Building a model is heavy work. The Geneva Association cites teams of 10 to 20 experts working over two to three years, with updates every 5 to 10 years [S14 p.27], and several million US dollars for the most advanced models, repeated every few years [S14 p.26]. Most insurers therefore licence: "most insurers have been licensing catastrophe models from third party vendors" [S11 p.12], and privately owned models are "licensed under commercial terms" [S14 p.26]. Cost pushes some the other way. With set-up costs of millions of US dollars, "many primary insurance companies choose not to license models and rely instead on third-party consultancies", while "Larger primary insurers tend to license all the commercial models" [S14 p.27]. The American Academy of Actuaries frames the trade-off as reliance on a broker or other third party versus "significant investment in training, software, and hardware" to build expertise in-house [S10 p.38]. In practice, a 2026 California report obtains vendor output through Aon "under the terms of its license agreements" [S23 p.127].

Public models

Public models are rare. "There are very few publicly available complete catastrophe models"; Florida's Public Hurricane Loss Projection Model was funded by the state's Office of Insurance Regulation at a cost of USD 2.7 million as an alternative to commercial models for homeowners' rate setting [S14 p.26]. California is now funding one. SB 429 (Statutes of 2025) directs a state programme to fund "the development, demonstration, and deployment of a public wildfire catastrophe model" [S23 p.53]; a Request for Expertise issued on 25 March 2026 is "funded with $8.5 million" over three years [S23 pp.53–54].

Caveats. the landscape sources are 2015, 2018 and 2026 snapshots; confirm current vendor names, ownership and platform terms before relying on them. The broker route rests on one licence line [S23 p.127], and each public-model account on a single passage. The sources give no market shares, no hosted or cloud access, and no Moody's platform terms.

Takeaway

  1. Three commercial vendors long led, each with its own platform; the named firms differ between the 2015, 2018 and 2026 sources.
  2. Open and vendor-opened platforms, specialists, broker-built and public models now sit beside the vendors rather than replacing them.
  3. Access runs through licences, consultancies, brokers and, rarely, public models; check current names and terms before relying on any of this.

Technology by module

Machine learning and imagery are being added to individual catastrophe-model steps, above all exposure capture, where one non-vendor study measured the effect; hazard, damage and run-time uses are vendor-reported, and vendors describe a physics-based core that AI augments selectively.

Technology is entering catastrophe models step by step, not as a replacement. Only one result in these sources is quantified and independent of a vendor, and it concerns exposure. The other uses are vendor-reported, or come from a government assessment of forecasting models, so this chapter grades each claim by its evidence.

Exposure capture: the measured case

Address-based geocoding usually yields "a single point location that may or may not accurately represent the building(s) to be insured" [S04 p.4]. A building footprint is a polygon giving a structure's size, shape and location, and remote sensing can delineate footprints across large areas [S04 p.7]. A Milliman study for the CAS and SOA (April 2025) compared the parcel centroid with a machine-learning footprint location for single-family homes in Hillsborough County, Florida. The two points were "more than ten meters apart for a majority (68%) of homes", in a combined dataset of 337,460 homes [S04 pp.4, 11].

Elevation explains why this matters for water. The two locations often sat at different elevations, which can significantly drive flood risk [S04 p.4]. Where the footprint location was more than three feet lower, "the average storm surge expected loss was over three times higher at the footprint-derived location", and a similar result held when the difference was reversed [S04 p.4].

That is a change in modelled loss, not a measured gain in accuracy. The analysis used KatRisk's SpatialKat flood and storm-surge models, and "An analysis based on different catastrophe models would likely produce a different result" [S04 p.42]. The authors call for future case studies to "evaluate the relative accuracy and benefit of various data sources" [S04 p.40]. Location also matters unevenly: hurricane wind risk "is not expected to vary over a distance of several dozen meters", whereas flood and wildfire can vary over short distances, so for many perils "knowing exact structure location is not significantly important" [S04 p.38].

Vendor-reported uses, module by module

Exposure attributes. Moody's (February 2026) describes computer vision and machine learning delivering "property-level risk attributes, roof condition, vegetation coverage, and defensible space at scale" [S09 p.7]. A 2020 Verisk pilot trained a convolutional neural network on known high-rise buildings and ran it over 10-metre Sentinel-2 imagery of South Korea and China; the method "yields a manageable number of cells that can be manually reviewed" by an analyst [S15 p.6].

Hazard. Verisk (May 2026) says AI is used to "synthesize storm footprints and rainfall events, and predict wildfire spread dynamics" [S05 p.3].

Damage and vulnerability. After the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires, Moody's describes AI classifying structures as "fully destroyed, partially damaged, or even untouched", "strengthening the accuracy of early loss guidance and model validation" [S09 pp.3–4]. Verisk describes AI extracting structural characteristics, defensible-space conditions and surrounding vegetation [S05 p.3].

Run time. Verisk says AI "dramatically accelerates simulation of large stochastic event sets", without a stated speed-up [S05 p.3]. GAO's "about 1,000 times faster" figure is an emulator of a weather-model component that can take 30 to 80 percent of a traditional model's computation time; emulators "initially require extensive training data, computational resources for that training data, and expertise to develop", so the effort moves upfront into training [S13 p.26].

Limits, with scope

GAO's December 2023 assessment covers machine learning in forecasting models for severe storms, hurricanes, floods and wildfires. It excluded "catastrophe models used for insurance company risk management" [S13 p.54], so its limits transfer only by analogy. It reports that machine learning "is often unable to extrapolate from its training data to new situations" [S13 p.34], and that ML models "may perform worse than traditional models in forecasting high-intensity hurricanes because there are not enough data for them to train on" [S13 p.33]. On wildfire, "the majority of machine learning applications to wildfire modeling have been for model inputs" [S13 p.35]. Moody's states its own limit: AI "does not directly price risk" [S09 p.9].

GAO reports one company that cut computational cost "by completely replacing a traditional flood model with a machine learning model" [S13 p.34]; that was a flood forecasting model at a private company, not an insurer's catastrophe model.

A physics-based core, augmented selectively

Verisk says the core of its catastrophe models "remains grounded in physics", with AI introduced "selectively to augment well-established physical and statistical representations" [S05 p.2]. Maturity statements come from different years and cover different things, so they are not set against each other here. In 2023 GAO reported, citing agency officials, that "most of the efforts to use machine learning for hazard models are still in research and development" [S13 p.25]. In 2026 Verisk said its AI is applied "not as part of a pilot program or proof of concept, but as an integral component of our catastrophe models" [S05 p.3]. Read together, the sources support additions to individual steps, above all exposure capture.

Caveats. only the footprint study is quantified and non-vendor, and it measures modelled-loss change, not accuracy; its 68% and three-times figures appear in the Executive Summary only. Vendor posts are self-reported. GAO covers forecasting, not insurer models. No source shows an insurer model wholly replaced by ML or gives a run-time baseline.

Takeaway

  1. The one measured result concerns exposure: ML footprints moved modelled surge loss, and accuracy was not tested.
  2. Hazard, damage and run-time uses are vendor-reported; GAO's limits come from forecasting models, not insurer models.
  3. Vendors describe AI added selectively to a physics-based core; the 2023 and 2026 maturity statements describe different things.

What still breaks

Data gaps, non-modelled and secondary perils, a non-stationary climate and opacity remain open, and AI adds validation and explainability problems; the sources describe only partial, proposed or self-reported responses, none shown to be adopted widely or effective.

Each challenge below is paired with a response and the limit that remains, and dated, because several responses are old. None of the sources shows any response to be adopted widely or to work.

Exposure data gaps

Challenge (2008). An RMS post-mortem of 2004 claims indicated that "data quality issues contributed up to 45% of the gap between modelled and actual incurred losses" [S03 p.12]. Response. The 2008 guide proposed "A simple yet comprehensive framework for exposure data quality assessment" [S03 p.14]. Limit. "there is currently no accepted industry standard to follow" [S03 p.14].

The problem persists in 2026. California's SB 254 study says "insurability decisions are already being made with incomplete data" [S23 p.54], and proposes a Statewide Data Commons to "systematically collect and store previously inaccessible wildfire mitigation and suppression data" [S23 p.53]. That is a proposal, with an upfront cost for data, technology and architecture [S23 p.54].

Non-modelled and secondary perils

Challenge (2024). In Australia, bushfire, flood, storm and hail are known as non-modelled perils because vendors have not traditionally modelled them; some now do, but these models "have not been universally accepted" [S22 pp.18–19]. Secondary perils in the cascading sense, such as storm surge, liquefaction and fire following earthquake, may also be inadequately allowed for in modelled results, so adjustment factors may be needed [S22 p.8]. Response. "an allowance for losses in respect of these perils would need to be added to the Natural Perils (NP) PML" [S22 p.19]. Limit. "It is unlikely to be possible to develop a single methodology for capturing non-modelled perils", so a material exposure calls for understanding the method and parameters used [S22 p.13].

Non-stationary climate

Challenge (2018). "The topic of non-stationarity in the peril is a key challenge for risk assessment" [S14 p.23]. Response. Vendors "have taken some steps to produce different versions of their models calibrated to different periods of history", for example to reflect the multi-decadal oscillation in hurricane frequency or a probabilistic forecast of hurricane activity over the next five years [S14 p.23]. Limit. Their reliability for placement and risk transfer is "hotly debated within the industry" [S14 p.23]. No source here describes a newer response; a 2024 Australian paper calls climate-change assessment "an emerging area with increasing focus" [S22 p.3].

Opacity

Challenge. "some core assumptions are considered proprietary and are not readily accessible to users" [S10 p.38] (2018). For machine learning, the complexity of some techniques, such as deep learning, "can make determining how they make predictions nearly impossible" [S13 p.39] (2023, forecasting models). Response. "Greater transparency is essential to enable buy-in, review and challenge by other interested parties" [S14 p.38]. Limit. Disclosure must stay "sensitive to commercial considerations around investment in intellectual property" [S14 p.38]. The authors of a February 2026 US flood comparison still conclude that "Further transparency of catastrophe model outputs and methodologies is necessary" [S16 p.1].

Explainable or not? Verisk says "Physics is a prerequisite for AI outputs to be explainable to internal stakeholders, defensible to regulators" [S05 p.3]; GAO reports agencies saying private companies "do not provide them with the details of their machine learning models" [S13 p.40]. The first is a vendor's account of its own models, the second concerns forecasting models.

AI validation and explainability

Challenge (2023). Agency officials told GAO there is "no standardized or codified process for assessing machine learning models" [S13 p.44], and GAO's report relays a framework's note that explainable AI is "difficult to develop and incorporate" [S13 p.41]. Response. GAO's policy options include that policymakers "could develop guidelines or best practices for documenting and publicly reporting training data choices" [S13 p.49]; these are options, not adopted measures [S13 p.4]. Verisk (May 2026) lists its own safeguards: "physics-based guardrails, expert validation, cross-peril consistency checks, and transparent documentation of every assumption" [S05 p.4], which are self-reported. Limit. GAO cautions that embedding bias-mitigation efforts across a model's life cycle "may increase model costs and slow model development" [S13 p.49].

Governance and model risk

Response (2024). The Australian paper says good practice requires "a governance framework to ensure that the data used is reviewed appropriately, is complete, accurate and consistent" [S22 p.5]. Model choice is itself a risk: "An analysis based on different catastrophe models would likely produce a different result" [S04 p.42] (2025). Response (2018). "There are multiple viable approaches as to how to handle model uncertainty, including model adjustments and model blending" [S14 p.26]. Limit. The weights in a blend add assumptions that "may add to, rather than reduce, result uncertainty" [S22 p.9].

None of these responses is shown, in these sources, to be adopted widely or to work. GAO's are policy options, the Australian and Geneva Association items are guidance or proposals, and Verisk's are self-reported.

Caveats. responses are dated, and 2008 and 2018 items may have been superseded; nothing newer on non-stationarity appears in these sources. The AI evidence is GAO's on forecasting models, not insurer models, plus vendor statements. The 45% figure comes from one 2008 post-mortem.

  2. Each response is partial, proposed, an allowance or self-reported, and the dates run from 2008 to 2026.
  3. No source shows any response adopted widely or effective.

Glossary

30 terms, defined in the guide’s own words with the best source passage for each. “Guide definition” marks terms the sources use but do not define.

AAL (average annual loss)
The average of a portfolio's simulated yearly losses: the expected loss in a typical year and the usual base for catastrophe pricing. It is the same idea as pure premium. [S12 p.19]
AEP (aggregate exceedance probability)
The chance that the sum of all event losses in a year exceeds a given amount. At any return period it reads at or above the OEP, because a year can contain several events. [S22 p.17; S22 p.19]
Aggregate cover
Cover that responds to the total of a year's losses rather than to any single event, such as an annual stop-loss. The AEP is the curve used to assess it. (guide definition — no defining passage in the sources; term used at [S22 p.17])
Aleatory uncertainty
The inherent randomness of the hazard process itself, which better data or a better model cannot remove. [S11 p.11]
Attachment point
The loss amount at which a layer of cover or a backstop starts to pay. One source defines it for a state-sponsored backstop as the aggregate loss that triggers payment; another uses it for reinsurance terms. [S23 p.97; S17 p.21]
Blending
Producing one estimate from several models' results, usually by weighting them. The weights are extra assumptions. [S18 p.5; S22 p.9]
Burn rate
Average annual loss per US$1,000 of coverage, a way to compare loss levels across models and across portfolios of different size. [S16 p.9]
Cascading perils
Hazards triggered by, or following, a main event, such as storm surge after a hurricane or fire following earthquake. The sources usually call these secondary perils. [S22 p.8; S14 p.30]
Damage function / vulnerability curve
A relationship that turns hazard intensity into expected damage for a type of structure, often as a share of replacement value. It is built from claims data, engineering methods or both. [S12 p.15; S11 p.6; S14 p.23]
Demand surge
A sudden, usually temporary rise in the cost of materials, services and labour after a catastrophe, because many owners need repairs at once. It is also called post-loss inflation. [S22 p.18]
EP curve (exceedance probability curve)
A curve giving, for each loss size, the probability that a portfolio's loss in a year exceeds it. It can be drawn on an occurrence basis or an aggregate basis. [S12 p.19]
Epistemic uncertainty
Uncertainty from incomplete knowledge of a process's parameters or of the right model structure. Estimating it takes several models built with different parameterisations. [S11 p.11]
Event catalog / event set
The database of simulated scenario events a model runs. Each event carries a size or strength, a location, a path and an annual probability, or rate. [S12 p.15]
Event loss table (ELT)
The model's list of simulated events with their loss amounts and, usually, their frequencies. [S12 p.13; S22 p.18]
Exhaustion
The loss level at which a layer's limit is used up and the layer stops paying. (guide definition — no defining passage in the sources; term used at [S12 p.20])
Exposure data
The database of in-force characteristics of what is exposed to loss: locations, values, building attributes and policy terms. It is a key model input. [S22 p.3; S22 p.5]
Footprint
In hazard modelling, the map of local intensity (wind speed, water depth, ground shaking) that one event produces. In exposure work, a building footprint is the polygon giving a structure's size, shape and location. [S12 p.15; S10 p.4; S04 p.7]
Geocoding
Turning an address into a map location, usually a single point that may not represent the building well. [S04 p.4]
Ground-up, gross and net loss
Ground-up loss is damage before limits and deductibles; gross is the loss after deductibles, limits and attachment points; net is the loss after reinsurance recoveries. [S17 p.21]
Loss amplification
Post-event factors that push final losses above direct physical damage; the source's first example is economic demand surge. [S14 p.30]
Non-modelled perils
Perils that matter to an insurer but that its models do not cover. In Australia, bushfire, flood, storm and hail were named because vendors had not traditionally modelled them. [S22 pp.18–19]
OEP (occurrence exceedance probability)
The chance that the largest single event loss in a year exceeds a given amount. It is the curve behind per-occurrence reinsurance layers. [S22 p.19; S12 p.19]
PML (probable maximum loss)
The loss at a chosen exceedance probability or return period, such as the 1-in-200 loss read from the OEP curve. It is an amount expected to be exceeded with a stated probability, not a cap on possible loss. [S22 p.19; S10 p.15]
Primary and secondary uncertainty
Primary uncertainty concerns the events themselves: whether and how often they occur. Secondary uncertainty concerns the range of loss amounts once an event has occurred. [S17 p.39; S22 p.19; S11 p.7]
Reinstatement
Restoring a layer's limit after a loss so the cover is available again for later events. (guide definition — no defining passage in the sources; term used at [S22 p.17])
Return period
The reciprocal of the annual exceedance probability. A 100-year return period means one such event or worse per 100 years on average, not one every 100 years. [S12 p.19; S22 p.19]
Secondary perils
In this guide, the frequent perils whose models are less mature (severe convective storm, wildfire, flood, hail), set against hurricane and earthquake. The sources imply this split but do not define it, and mostly use the term for cascading perils. (guide definition — no defining passage in the sources; split implied at [S12 p.9])
TVaR / CTE (tail value at risk; conditional tail expectation)
The average loss given that the loss exceeds a chosen threshold, so it reflects the thickness of the tail rather than one point on the curve. One source spells CTE as "Conditional Tail Exceedance". [S17 p.39; S11 p.14]
View of risk
In this guide, an insurer's documented, calibrated basis for its own portfolio: the model choices, adjustments, blend weights and reasons behind the loss numbers it relies on. (guide definition — no defining passage in the sources; the phrase is used loosely at [S14 p.27; S11 p.11; S17 p.37])
Year loss table (YLT)
A table of simulated years with the loss falling in each, as against the event-by-event listing of an event loss table. (guide definition — no defining passage in the sources; named beside the event loss table at [S12 p.13])
